\section{Introduction}

\section{Sample Selection}

The sample used to compare to \citet{McQuillan14} was drawn from the
APOGEE Data Release 14\footnote{\url{http://www.sdss.org/dr14/irspec/}}.
The dwarfs were chosen from three specific targeting programs:
\texttt{APOGEE_KEPLER_COOLDWARF}, \texttt{APOGEE2_APOKASC_DWARF}, and
\texttt{APOGEE_KEPLER_SEISMO} (with APOGEE \(\logg > 3.5\)). The former
two classes were chosen strictly on stellar parameters, and should not
be biased toward rotation. The latter was specifically chosen for
asteroseismic measurements, and thus is biased against rapid rotators.
We will treat that separately.

\subsection{Cool Dwarf Sample}

This sample was chosen to obtain spectra for around 1200 cool dwarfs
with long- and short-cadence data from
\Kepler\footnote{\url{http://www.sdss.org/dr13/algorithms/ancillary/apogee/kepler_dwarfs/}}.
The stellar parameter criteria for this sample are that objects should
have original KIC cuts of \(\Teff < 5500\) K, \(\logg > 4.0\) and 2MASS
\(7 < H < 11\) mag. These targets are shown in red in
\cref{fig:targetingsamples}. Due to the limited quality of the original
KIC stellar parameter determinations \gvs{Number of giants} turned out
to be giants.

\subsection{APOKASC Dwarfs}

This sample was chosen based on the revised parameters from
\citet{Huber14}. The selection criteria were to have \citet{Huber14} \(6500 \ge
\Teff \ge 5000\) K, \(\logg > 3.5\), and \(7 < H < 11\) mag. These
targets are shown in blue in \cref{fig:targetingsamples}.

\section{Background contamination}

There are many cases a rotation modulation may be detected, but not
originate in the target star. A listing of the common ones are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Due to the wide \Kepler{} Point Spread Function, light from an unassociated,
    nearby rapidly-rotating late K or M dwarf could be captured in the
    aperture. These objects should show Variability-Induced Motion
    (VIM) \authorcomment1{What is the background level of VIM?}, and the companion
    should generally be evident in high-resolution imaging. Due to the
    low density of objects in the \Kepler{} field, these should be
    relatively rare \authorcomment1{How uncommon?}. 
  \item The target may be part of a wide binary with a rapidly-rotating
    K or M dwarf as a companion. Based on a quick sampling from the
    binary mass-function, pairings where the secondary is a K or M dwarf
    are not uncommon. And based on angular momentum evolution models, a
    small fraction of these companions should have initial conditions
    enabling them to maintain their rapid rotation for several Gyr.
  \item The target may be part of a close binary with a rapidly-rotating
    K or M dwarf as a companion. \authorcomment1{These systems may or may not be
      more common because of two competing effects---since the companion
      will become tidally-synchronized, that means ALL companions in
      orbits with periods less than the tidal-synchronization period
      should be rapid rotators, not just a tail of the initial
      conditions, but on the other hand, the fraction of short-period
      systems is small. I'm not sure how comparable those fractions
    are.} However, due to tidal synchronization, these two should yield
    concordant \vsini{} and photometric modulations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\vsini{} comparison}

One way we investigated whether the photometric variability was
associated with rapid rotation was by comparing the photometric period
to the spectroscopic \vsini. Objects with large \vsini{} values should
have correspondingly short periods.

One of the major uncertainties in relating rotational periods to
expected \vsini{}s is the radius, which acts as a scale factor. We chose
radii from the Kepler Stellar Properties
Catalog\footnote{\url{https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/docs/Kepler_stellar_docs.html}},
which reports radii derived based on the methodology of \citep{Huber14}.

\subsubsection{Asteroseismic Dwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Asteroseismic_radii_vsini_check}
  \caption{\emph{Left:} ASPCAP \vsini{} compared to the inferred
equatorial velocity for APOKASC dwarfs with vsini detections. 5/27
objects have \vsini{} values larger than the inferred equatorial
velocities. \emph{Right:} Asteroseismic radii compared to radii inferred from
\vsini{} and the rotation period. The points above the line are the same
as the points above the line on the left.\label{fig:astero_radii}}
\end{figure}

As a starting point, we used a sample of dwarfs with accurate radii
measured from asteroseismology in the APOKASC sample. There are 426
dwarfs with asteroseismic radii in the APOKASC catalog v3.6.5. 93 of
these dwarfs have periods in \citet{McQuillan14}. To obtain a
high-quality sample for further study, we removed all objects with any
of the STAR\_WARN, STAR\_BAD, or VSINI\_WARN flags in the latest DR14 catalog, 
leaving a sample of 77 objects. All 77 APOGEE spectra were inspected by eye 
for double-lined spectra, which excluded two
(2M19252467+4207248/KIC6689943 \& 2M19034695+4055143/KIC5689219) objects. 
None of the final sample of 75 objects are known \Kepler{} pulsators. 

Of the remaining APOKASC sample with \citet{McQuillan14} periods, 27 have 
\vsini{} detections (\(\vsini > 7\) km/s) using DR14 values. The 
correspondence between the expected and observed velocities for the objects 
with \vsini{} detections are shown in \cref{fig:astero_radii}. As expected, 
most of the stars lie below the equality line, corresponding to \(\sin i \le 
1\).  There are five objects which have unphysical solutions, which are shown
in \cref{table:bad_astero}.

\input{tables/bad_astero.tex}

Of these five objects, three (KIC 8677016, 8952800, 10518551) have
\vsini{} values close to the detection threshold. The probability
distribution function of marginal \vsini{} detections are complex, so
it may be possible that these are outliers. KIC 3223000 has a
\vsini{} which is slightly above the predicted equatorial velocity. It
is a 1.7-\(\sigma\) difference based on the \vsini{} uncertainty, which
is consistent with being a statistical fluctuation. Additionally, it can
be explained by 4.9 day measured rotation period actually being a 
half-period detection alias, implying that the real equatorial velocity to be twice 
as large. 

The final outlier, KIC 9908400, is so significantly off of the relation
that it can not be explained by either uncertainties or aliasing. However,
it may be adequately explained by contamination. With a high
temperature, it could be a rapidly rotating star located above the Kraft 
break. If the photometric variation originated in a cooler contaminant within 
the Kepler PSF, then the mismatch between the period and \vsini{} would
be explained.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Velocity_vsini_APOKASC_apogeeteffs}
  \caption{Comparison of inferred equatorial velocities and
    \vsini{} across effective temperature for asteroseismic dwarfs. Blue
  points are predicted equatorial velocities while red diamonds are
  measured \vsini{} values from DR14. If the equatorial velocity is
  greater than \vsini{}, then the two points are joined by a black line.
If the points imply \(\sin i > 1\), then they are joined by a red line.
The detection threshold of 7 km/s is shown as the red dashed line.
For 28 objects, both the predicted equatorial velocities and the measured

\end{figure}

To see if this mismatch is prevalent among the hot stars, we looked to
see if the velocity mismatch trends with effective temperature.
\Cref{fig:velmatch_teff} compares the predicted equatorial velocities to
\vsini{} for objects with varying effective temperatures. There's no
apparent trend where hotter stars tend to have more mismatches. However,
this does not preclude KIC 9908400 from being a contaminant if this sort
of contamination is rare.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{apokasc_vsini_comparison}
  \caption{\emph{Top:} Observed distribution of \vsini{} (red) compared
  to the theoretical distribution of \vsini{} (black). The theoretical
distribution was obtained by taking the inferred equatorial velocity
distribution (blue) and convolving it with \(\sin i\) and a 10\%
uncertainty. \vsini{} non-detections are treated separately; out of 75 
objects, 43 non-detections were predicted and 42 were observed.
\emph{Bottom:} Cumulative distribution of \vsini{} including the impact
of non-detections. Small statistical blips are seen around 15 and 20
km/s, but according to a KS test, these two distributions are consistent
with being identical (\(p=0.5\))\label{fig:apokasc_vdists}}
\end{figure}

The overall distribution of \vsini{} is consistent with the inferred
velocity distribution. This was tested by taking the inferred velocity
distribution, convolving it with a Gaussian with a width of 10\%, and
then convolving that distribution with a distribution of
randomly-oriented \(\sin i\). The results are shown in
\cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}. As expected, the number of targets in each
\vsini{} bin tracks the theoretical prediction, including non-detections. To 
quantify this agreement, a KS-test indicated that the \vsini{}s were 
consistent with being drawn from the theoretical distribution at the
\(p=0.5\) confidence level. I will note that the  convolutions were performed 
on the binned dataset as opposed to the full dataset, which introduced 
integration errors on the order of 2\%. However, decreasing the bin sizes 
didn't affect the conclusion.

While the overall distribution of \vsini{} matches the distribution of
velocities, the previous test didn't address whether \vsini{} were
individually consistent with velocities. That would require checking the 
distribution of \(\sin i\), which hasn't been adequately done yet.
However, it's on the list.

\subsubsection{Cool Dwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{McQuillan_APOGEE_hr}
  \caption{HR diagram using \citet{Huber14} parameters for the sample
  overlapping \citet{McQuillan14} period detections and APOGEE DR14
targets with good fits. For temperatures cooler than 5450 K, the
subgiant branch (blue stars) is clearly separated from the main sequence
branch (red circles).}
  \label{fig:cool_subgiants}
\end{figure}

Since the asteroseismic stars were promising, we decided to look into
another regime where radii should be relatively well-constrained, which
is the cool-star limit. We define the cool star regime as the region in
the HR-diagram of \citet{Huber14} parameters, where the subgiant branch for
stars older than the field turnoff are well-separated from the
main-sequence. The separation is prominent for \(T < 5450\) K with a
dividing line at \(\log g = 4.3\), as illustrated in 
\cref{fig:cool_subgiants}. As before, double-lined spectroscopic
binaries and pulsators were removed, in addition to objects with either
the STAR_WARN or STAR_BAD flags. This leaves 234 targets in this range, of 
which only 6 are rapid rotators (1 day < P < 5 day). 

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{cool_stars_velocity_radius}
  \caption{\emph{Left:} ASPCAP \vsini{} compared to the inferred
equatorial velocity for cool APOKASC dwarfs with vsini detections. 5/27
objects have \vsini{} values larger than the inferred equatorial
velocities. \emph{Right:} Asteroseismic radii compared to radii inferred from
\vsini{} and the rotation period. The points above the line are the same
as the points above the line on the left.\label{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}}
\end{figure}

Taking an initial look at the conformance between \vsini{}, period, and
radius indicates disagreement, as seen in
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}. For a large number of objects
the \vsini{} is too large given the observed period and radius. The
disagreement is significantly more severe for the dwarfs than the
subgiants. Therefore, at least one of the three quantities needed to
make \cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}: radius, \vsini{}, or
rotation period, should be wrong.

One of the obvious places where things can go wrong is with the stellar
radii. Subgiants misclassified as dwarfs would have equatorial
velocities that are severely underestimated by the \citet{Huber14}
pipeline. While misclassified subgiants wouldn't be able to explain the
objects with very large discrepancies in
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, it could plausibly be behind the 
objects with \(R\sin i \sim 2 R_\sun\).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{cool_apogee_logg_comparison}
  \caption{Dwarfs and subgiants classified by \citet{Huber14} placed in
  an HR diagram with APOGEE spectroscopic parameters. Most of the 
  \citet{Huber14} dwarfs (blue crosses) are on the APOGEE dwarf locus.
  The \citet{Huber14} subgiants (red diamonds) are found both in the
  subgiant branch as well as the dwarf locus. \vsini{} detections are
  denoted as squares, with a magenta fill indicating a \citet{Huber14}
  subgiant and a white fill indicating a \citet{Huber14} 
  dwarf.\label{fig:cool_apogee_logg_comparison}}
\end{figure}

In order to determine whether this misclassification could be an issue,
we compare the dwarf/subgiant classification from \citet{Huber14} to
that which would be obtained from APOGEE spectroscopic \logg{}
measurements. While the spectroscopic \logg{} measurements for dwarfs
are uncalibrated, and should not be taken at face value, we assume that
they should be able to distinguish between subgiants and dwarfs, at the
very least. The APOGEE spectroscopic measurements are shown in
\cref{fig:cool_apogee_logg_comparison}. Due to the uncalibrated nature
of the APOGEE \logg{}s, the main sequence does not behave as expected,
particularly at the cool end. However, a clear main-sequence and
subgiant branch can be distinguished. The APOGEE measurements indicate
the surprising result that it is not the subgiants that are
misclassified as dwarfs by \citet{Huber14}; rather APOGEE indicates that
dwarfs are being misclassified as subgiants. This exacerbates the
problem of the radii being underestimated.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{APOGEE_Stauffer_Hartmann_Comparison_Good}
  \caption{Comparison between the overlap between APOGEE observations
    and those made in \citet{Stauffer87}. The red dashed lines indicate
  the detection limits for the two measurements. Objects which are
nondetections in both surveys are omitted. Above the detection limits,
the two \vsini{} measurements are in good agreement.}
  \label{fig:APOGEE_Stauffer_Hartmann_Comparison_Good}
\end{figure}

Since subgiant contamination is ruled out as highly unlikely, then we
next consider the situation that \vsini{} values are incorrect.
\vsini{} measurement was only added in the previous data release DR13,
and has not been vetted and calibrated to the extent of the other stellar
parameters. In particular, degeneracy between \vsini{} and
\logg{} may make \vsini{} values for cool dwarfs particularly
unreliable. We check this by comparing the APOGEE \vsini{} values to
measured \vsini{} values for cool dwarfs in the Pleiades
\citep{Stauffer87} in
\cref{fig:APOGEE_Stauffer_Hartmann_Comparison_Good}.
