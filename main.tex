\section{Introduction}

One of the significant results from the \Kepler{} satellite has been the
determination of rotational periods for a large sample of old, field stars
\citep{Basri11,Affer12,Nielsen13,Reinhold13,McQuillan14,Garcia14}.
These rotation rates are expected to be extremely useful to deriving a field
gyrochronology, which can lead to a better characterization of the ages of
\Kepler{} stars, and hence a better understanding of the discovered planets.

The \Kepler{} field was designed to point outside of the plane of the galactic
disk, making the field biased toward old stars. Despite the old age of the
field, there is still a population of rapid rotators at the level of 1\% of the
population which have rotation rates significantly higher than expected from
existing gyrochronological relations. One possible explanation for these rapid
rotators are that they are tidally-synchronized binaries. The synchronized
binaries are useful because they provide insight into the formation of binaries
at scales too small for disk fragmentation to occur. 

While there have been many attempts to evaluate the determination of rotation
periods in stars throughout the \Kepler{} field, they have all been through
comparisons between different periodogram analysis, but not with independent 
measures of rotation, such as \vsini.

One of the valuable tools in performing this analysis is a uniform derivation
of the radii of these targets. The initial attempt at characterizing stars and
providing radii was the original KIC \citep{Brown11}, which fit broad-band
\textit{griz} and an intermediate-band \textit{D51} photometry to
\citet{Castelli04} stellar atmospheres and \citet{Girardi00} isochrones.
High-resolution spectroscopy found that radii from the original KIC were found 
to be systematically underestimated \citep{Everett13}. Additionally, around 5\%
of \Kepler{} targets had KIC parameters which were considered unphysical for an 
old field population \citep{Batalha13}. 

\citet{Huber14}, and the updated methodology in \citep{Mathur17} attempt to
address the shortcomings in the original stellar parameter classification by
re-adjusting the KIC parameters to conform to more current DSEP isochrones 
\citep{Dotter08}, and also used more precise input stellar parameters such as
asteroseismology and spectroscopy. In addition to refitting the original KIC 
stellar parameters, \citet{Huber14} derives stellar parameters for the 7\% of 
observed \Kepler{}  
targets without KIC stellar parameters due to missing photometric measurements. 
\citet{Huber14} classified these stars using 
asteroseismology, 2MASS colors, and \Kepler-INT Sloan photometry
\citep{Greiss12}. Because
the APOGEE-2 targeting program \citep{Zasowski17} was based on \citep{Huber14}
stellar parameters without regard to their presence in the original KIC, and
serendipitously observed many of these missing targets, providing a check on
how successful the \citet{Huber14} methodology worked in the absence of full
photometry, 

In this paper, we look at the rotation distribution of \Kepler{} targets as
seen from APOGEE \vsini{}s. APOGEE's limited resolution will only make it
sensitive to the most rapid rotators, particularly on the cool end. However,
this is exactly the population we're interested in. 

\section{Catalog Data}
\label{sec:sample}

The base sample for our study of rotation in the \Kepler{} field is the 
APOKASC sample \citep{Pinsonneault12}. APOGEE spectra provide a complementary
view of rotation to the photometric rotation periods derived by
\Kepler{} \citep{McQuillan14}. In addition to the measures of rotation, we
also use astrometric and photometric information to derive radii for our 
sample. All quantities will be described in more detail in the sections
below.

\subsection{APOGEE}
\label{sec:APOGEE}

The underlying sample is drawn from the Data Release 14 \citep{Abolfathi18}
of the APOGEE survey \citep{Majewski17}. The workhorse behind the survey is the
APOGEE spectrograph, a high-resolution (\(R \sim 22,000\)) multi-fiber
near-infrared spectrograph \citep{Wilson10} mounted on the SDSS 2.5-meter
telescope at Apache Point Observatory \citep{Gunn06}.

Reduction of the APOGEE data takes place in three main stages. First,
individual visit spectra are reduced, including detector calibration, bad pixel
masking, wavelength calibration, sky subtraction, and determination of
individual radial velocities through cross-correlation of template spectra.
Second, the individual spectra are combined by correcting for radial velocity
differences between the exposures, either by cross-correlating with each other,
or with template spectra, whichever leads to a smaller scatter
\citep{Holtzman18}. The full process is detailed in \citet{Nidever15}.

The final step is the extraction of stellar parameters and chemical abundances
by the APOGEE Stellar Parameter and Chemical Abundances Pipeline (ASPCAP)
\citep{GarciaPerez16}. ASPCAP measures stellar parameters by performing a
chi-squared minimization \citep{AllendePrieto06} over a 6-dimensional space of
\Teff, \logg, [M/H], [\(\alpha\)/M], \vsini, and microturbulent velocity. Once
those six parameters have been determined, ASPCAP measures abundances for
individual elements, including \feh{}.

After determination of the stellar parameters and chemical abundances, the 
effective temperatures are calibrated to the photometric system of 
\citet{GonzalezHernandez09} using a metallicity-dependent offset, and the 
abundances are calibrated to provide homogeneous results within clusters. 
After calibration, the scatter between the 
calibrated and photometric temperatures  were found to be a function of 
\Teff{}, ranging from 130 K at 5500 K down to 85 K at 4000 K
\citep{Holtzman18}. The statistical uncertainty in \feh{} was measured to be 
0.009 dex \citep{Holtzman18}, but the systematic uncertainty is likely closer
to 0.1 dex \citep{Serenelli17}.

The APOGEE \vsini{} measurements just became included as catalog values in DR14.
\vsini{} was originally included in ASPCAP as of DR13. As a result, they have
experienced only very light validation against existing rotation measurements
\citep{Tayar15}. We undertake a more rigorous validation in this work (see
Section~\ref{sec:vsinival}) and extend it to the cool dwarf domain.

To form the base sample, we select all \Kepler{} stars which were observed by
APOGEE for science programs as of DR14. The list of targeting flags we accepted
were: \APOGEECOOLDWARF, \APOKASC, \APOGEEEB, \APOGEEKOI,
\APOGEEKOICONTROL, \APOGEESEISMO, \APOGEETWOSEISMO, \APOGEERVMONITOR, 
\APOGEETWOEB, and \APOGEEHOST. \APOGEECOOLDWARF{} targets are bright and were 
selected to have KIC \(\Teff < 5500\) K and KIC
\(\logg > 4.0\). \APOKASC{} targets were selected based on \citet{Huber14}
\Teff{} and \logg{} to potentially exhibit asteroseismic signals.
\APOGEESEISMO{} stars were targeted to follow up stars showing asteroseismic 
oscillations in \Kepler{}. \APOGEEEB{} and \APOGEETWOEB{} are small programs to
follow up \Kepler{} eclipsing binaries. \APOGEEKOI{}, \APOGEEKOICONTROL{},
\APOGEERVMONITOR{}, and \APOGEEHOST{} were selected to follow up
\Kepler{} planet host stars. For full details about the targeting in APOGEE, we
refer the reader to the APOGEE targeting page
\footnote{\url{https://www.sdss.org/dr14/irspec/targets/}} as well as
\citep{Zasowski17}. 

We inspected the fits to a set of spectra representative of targets flagged 
by the ASPCAP pipeline as having potential quality problems. A small fraction
(2.6\%) of targets have the \STARBAD{} quality flag enabled. This quality flag
is triggered when any of the model parameters hit a grid edge, the chi-square
of the fit is poor, the spectrum has poor signal to noise, or if the
color-temperature of the targets are substantially different from the
spectroscopic temperature. Visual inspection of a representative sample of 
spectra indicated that the flag was largely the result of poor subtraction or 
normalization of the spectrum in the pipeline, or due to poor model fits on the 
cool (\(\Teff < 4250 K\)) end. However, there is a small fraction of objects
where the \STARBAD{} flag was only triggered because the \vsini{} hit a grid
edge. We manually calculate stellar parameters for these stars
\citep{Holtzman18}, and treat those few targets as \vsini{} lower limits. The
remaining \STARBAD{} objects are excluded from the sample. A substantially 
larger fraction of targets (26\%) have the \STARWARN{} quality flag enabled.
This quality flag is triggered when any model parameters are within 1/2 a grid
spacing of the grid edge, the chi-square of the fit is high, the
signal-to-noise is less than ideal, or if the color-temperature of the targets
are moderately different from the ASPCAP \Teff.  Visual inspection of a 
representative sample of these spectra indicates that the fits reasonably 
resemble the underlying spectra. We include all targets with the \STARWARN{} 
quality flag enabled. 

One potentially concerning flag is the \ATMOSHOLEBAD{} flag, which is not
documented, but also does not trigger the \STARBAD{} flag. To check if this
flag is associated with any relevant quality problems, we visually inspected
a third of the 72 \Kepler{} targets with the \ATMOSHOLEBAD{} flag, but without 
the \STARBAD{} flag. All of the inspected stars had ASPCAP fits which appeared
to be reasonable. As a result, we do not exclude any of the
\ATMOSHOLEBAD{} targets.

Because we are interested in \vsini{} for this analysis, one potentially
significant class of contaminants are double-lined spectroscopic binaries
(DLSBs). The ASPCAP pipeline intrinsically assumes a single-star stellar 
atmosphere. If there are double-lines in the spectrum, it will attempt to fit 
an extremely broad single line profile on top of both lines, resulting in a 
spurious rapid rotator. In order to exclude these targets from the analysis, 
we inspected the spectra of all targets near the main sequence with \vsini{} 
greater than \(\vsini > 10 \kms\) (the detection threshold determined in
\cref{sec:vsinival}). If a given star showed signs of double-lined spectra
either in the combined spectrum, or in any of the individual visit spectra, it
was flagged as a DLSB and was not included in the \vsini{} analysis.

\subsection{Rotation Periods}
\label{sec:periods}

The main sample of rotation periods in this work comes from
\citet{McQuillan14}. The primary selection criteria for targets to be analyzed
in \citet{McQuillan14} was to distinguish between dwarfs and giants by using
the \Teff-\logg{} cuts advocated by \citet{Ciardi11}. The stellar parameters
considered by \citet{McQuillan14} came from the original KIC \citep{Brown11}, in 
contrast to the selection criteria of the \APOKASC{} targeting program in 
APOGEE, which selected based on \citet{Huber14} values. \citet{Huber14} made
estimates of \Teff{} and \logg{} based on 2MASS colors for an additional 
\gvs{Re-check number} stars that were omitted by \citet{Brown11} due to 
incomplete KIC photometry. In order to avoid confusion with other periodic
signals, \citep{McQuillan} removed known eclipsing binaries
\citep{Prsa11,Kirk14} and KOIs \citep{Akeson13}. They also impose a
\Teff{} limit of less than 6500 K to reduce confusion with pulsators. Their
final sample to be analyzed had 133,030 stars

\citet{McQuillan14} calculated rotation periods using an autocorrelation
function (ACF) based method. Once the first period peak was identified in the
ACF, they evaluated the significance of the peak using two main criteria. The
first was that the peak was persistent over most of the \Kepler{} light curve.
The second was that the amplitude of the peak was larger than a \Teff{} and
period-dependent threshold selected to compromise between real detections and
false positives. The result was a list of 34,030 stars with period detections,
as well as a list of 99,000 stars with no significant period detection.

In addition to the \citet{McQuillan14} periods, we also make use of surface 
rotation periods derived for a sample of 310 \Kepler{} stars showing
asteroseismic oscillations \citep{Garcia14}. Unlike the methodology of
\citet{McQuillan14}, which was based on the autocorrelation of light curves,
the analysis in \citet{Garcia14} is based on wavelets. We use \citet{Garcia14}
data both as an independent check on the \citet{McQuillan14} periods as well as
a basis dataset for the APOKASC asteroseismic sample.

\subsection{Astrometry}
\label{sec:astrometry}

The availability of high-quality parallaxes from the \Gaia{} mission's Data
Release 2 \citep{Gaia18} enables both the determination of
radii for the APOGEE sample, as well as evolutionary state classifications for
stars. The \Gaia{} DR2 performed a fully consistent single-star
5-parameter (\(\alpha\), \(\delta\), \(\mu_\alpha\), \(\mu_\delta\), \(\pi\))
solution to 1.3 billion sources over 22 months of observations
\citep{Lindegren18}. \gvs{If all APOGEE targets were in Gaia, then this is not
necessary. If so, explain further?} For targets which could not be adequately 
fit with a single-star 5-parameter solution, a 2-parameter (\(\alpha\), \(\delta\))
solution is performed instead \citep{Michalik15}. The criteria for a
2-parameter fall-back solution include targets with Gaia \(G > 21.0\) mag, 
having fewer than 6 visibility periods of observations, and an error ellipse 
larger than a magnitude-dependent limit in its largest dimension 
\citep{Lindegren18}.

We use the cross-matched database of \citet{Berger18b} to match \Kepler{} targets
against \Gaia{} DR2 detections.  \citet{Berger18b} cross-matched targets in 
the KSPC DR25 \citep{Mathur17}, with \Gaia{} DR2 sources using both position 
and \(G\)-band flux. Of the successfully cross-matched targets, 
\citet{Berger18b} excluded objects with fractional parallax errors greater 
than 0.2, \(\Teff < 3000\) K, \(\logg < 0.1\), and low quality 2MASS 
photometry. Of the 16,915 objects observed by DR14, 778 did not make it in the
catalog by \citet{Berger18b}. Of the 778, only 273 are consistent with not
being giants according to the formula in \citep{Holtzman18}. 46 of these
objects were simply not found in \Gaia{} DR2. The rest were excluded by the
\citet{Berger18b} quality cuts.  Because the 
targets in \citet{Berger18b} have high-quality parallaxes, we use the 
traditional formula for deriving distance modulus from parallax instead of 
the Bayesian method advocated by \citet{Luri18} to reduce computational 
complexity. We include a zero-point offset of 0.05 determined from comparisons
to asteroseismic radii \citep{Zinn18}.

\subsection{Photometry}
\label{sec:photometry}

In order to characterize our stellar sample, we choose to use
\Ks-band photometry to minimize the impact of extinction. \Ks-band 
apparent magnitudes were measured by the 2MASS survey \citep{Skrutskie06}, 
which uniformly performed profile-fit photometry and derived 
uncertainties for the full \Kepler{} field.  The typical \Ks-band 
photometric uncertainty for the APOGEE sample is 0.017 mag. A very small 
fraction of targets (0.3\%) of the 
APOGEE sample have 2MASS photometry flagged due to blending. In order to 
ensure that all the photometry in our sample is uniformly analyzed, we 
excluded these blended objects.

\subsection{Stellar Radii}
\label{sec:radii}
The APOKASC asteroseismic dwarf and subgiant sample
consists of 426 targets which presented solar-type oscillations in \Kepler{} 
short-cadence data \citep{Chaplin11}, and were observed in APOGEE DR14
\citep{Majewski17}. Using asteroseismic scaling relations, the radii
for these objects can be determined to an accuracy of around 3\%
\citep{Serenelli17}. Because the amplitude of solar-type oscillations increases
as stars evolve, the asteroseismic sample largely consists of subgiants.
There is also evidence that the asteroseismic sample may be biased against
rapid rotation from the nondetection of oscillations in rapidly-rotating
giants. \citep{Gaulme14,Tayar15}. 

We use the asteroseismic stars to form a well-studied test sample to ensure
that our methodology works, and to measure our ability to determine stellar
radii using \Gaia{} astrometry.

\section{Derived Quantities}
\label{sec:derived}

We use many of the catalog data to derive physical quantities of the stars,
particularly the radius. We use calculate radii using two main methods: direct 
lookups in stellar models, and through the Stefan-Boltzmann law. The details of
both methods are described below.

\subsection{Binarity}
\label{sec:binarity}

One of the challenges in attaining reliable radii for the full
\Kepler{} sample is pinning down the age. For the upper main sequence, the only 
detailed evolutionary state information available for dwarfs is the luminosity.
For stars only slightly evolved above the main sequence, the luminosity
evolution becomes degenerate with binarity, which is illustrated in
\cref{fig:targeting}.

In order to avoid complications due to binarity, we split the APOGEE dwarf
sample into two regimes: the cool dwarf sample, where all displacement above
the main sequence is due to binarity, and the evolved subgiant sample, where
the displacement above the main sequence is too large to be accounted for by
binarity. In the cool dwarf sample, radii can be inferred through
\Teff{} and \feh{} without concern for age; this simplification is not possible
on the upper main sequence.

\subsection{MIST Radius}
\label{sec:MISTR}

We derive radii for the \Kepler{} cool dwarf sample using models from version 
1.2 of the MESA Isochrones and Stellar Tracks (MIST) isochrones 
\citep{Dotter16,Choi16}. The MIST isochrones are based on the Modules for 
Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA) evolutionary tracks \citep{Paxton11,Paxton13,Paxton15}. 
evolutionary models. Bolometric corrections translating evolutionary tracks to
photometric bands were generated from ATLAS12/SYNTHE stellar atmosphere models
\citep{Kurucz70,93}. 

When directly calculating radii from MIST isochrones, we interpolate on a
1 Gyr isochrone given an object's \Teff{} and \feh{}. This yields the model's
radius.

\subsection{\Gaia{} Radii}
\label{sec:GaiaR}

In addition to isochrone-based radii, we calculate isochrone-independent radii
by making use of the K-band luminosity and temperature through the
Stefan-Boltzmann law. In order to calculate radii based on \Gaia{} parallaxes, 
we make use of the relation:

\begin{equation}
    \log R = \log L_\sun - 0.4 \left(\Ks +5 \log \left(\frac{\omega}{100
    \textrm{ mas }} \right) + BC(\Teff, \feh) - M_bol,\sun\right) - 4 \log
        \Teff - \log 4\pi\sigma
\end{equation}

where \Ks is the 2MASS \Ks-band flux, \(\omega\) is the \Gaia{} parallax,
\(BC\) is the bolometric correction as calculated by MIST, \Teff is the
effective temperature, \feh is the metallicity, and \(M_bol,\sun=4.74\) is the
absolute bolometric magnitude of the Sun. All of these quantities except for
the bolometric correction are available in the APOGEE/\Gaia/2MASS catalogs.

We calculate the bolometric correction by interpolating over \Teff{} in a solar
metallicity MIST isochrone. We ignore the impact of metallicity for two
reasons: that bolometric correction is only a weak function of metallicity, and
that MIST isochrones overestimate the variation of \Ks-band luminosity with
metallicity \citep{Simonian19}. The effect of metallicity on the MIST
bolometric correction is illustrated in \cref{fig:bc_figure}. The RMS between
the high and low-metallicity corrections is 0.017 mag. Because the MIST
isochrones overpredict the variation in \MK with metallicity, the actual
variability in the correction is likely to be smaller.

To gauge the systematic uncertainty in the MIST bolometric correction, we
compare the MIST solar-metallicity bolometric correction to the
solar-metallicity bolometric correction reported in \citep{Casagrande10}. We
select the \(BC_\Ks(V-\Ks)\) relation from their paper because it had the least
amount of scatter. The RMS between the two bolometric corrections is 0.01 mag
between the color range for which the \citep{Casagrande10} isochrone is
calibrated.

Another major systematic uncertainty when calculating radii through the K-band
luminosity is the effect of binarity. Binary systems are not well-described as
a single-temperature spherical blackbody; as a result, the radii calculated
from them will not be physical. As a first-order correction, we project the
luminosity down to the main sequence using a solar-metallicity MIST isochrone,
and then use the projected luminosity to calculate the radius of the primary.

\section{Regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}

The various measurements of stellar radius and rotation will have systematic
errors and selection effects that vary as a function of position on the HR
diagram. For example, the \Gaia{} radii are contaminated by photometric
binaries near the main sequence, but should accurately measure the radius far
above the main sequence, where an evolved primary substantially outshines a
main-sequence secondary. Additionally, there is not uniform analysis of
rotation periods in the subgiants because \citet{McQuillan14} attempted to
exclude them in their sample selection. In order to make rough determinations
of which methodologies will be better applicable for which subset of stars, we
split the HR diagram into five separate categories, shown in
\cref{fig:regimes}.

The cool dwarf regime is defined as the region near the main sequence where
stars have not evolved substantially compared to the age of the galaxy. As a
result, the spread in luminosity is either due to binarity or metallicity. We
base the temperature threshold (\(\Teff < 5250\) K) for the cool dwarf regime 
both on empirical measurements of the main sequence width, as well as 
predictions from stellar isochrones as discussed in \citet{Simonian19}. We draw
a luminosity threshold of 1.3 mag above a 1 Gyr solar metallicity MIST
isochrone. The luminosity threshold is cleanly separates the main-sequence from 
the base of the red giant branch (RGB).  Because of the high luminosity cut, 
this sample contains both single as well as binary systems, which we will 
further differentiate when needed. On the unevolved main sequence, the most
appropriate radius can be calculated as described in Section~\ref{sec:MISTR}
since age evolution is not a concern.

The hot dwarf regime is defined as the region where age and binarity are
degenerate, at \(\Teff > 5250\) K and located less than 1.3 mag above a 1 Gyr
solar metallicity MIST isochrone. In this regime it is ambiguous whether a
star more luminous than the main sequence is an evolved single star, or a 
phometric binary that should be deprojected. Because of this fundamental
ambiguity, we do not attempt to calculate radii in this regime.

Hot stars at higher luminosities---1.3--2.2 mag above the main 
sequence---are classified as subgiants. The subgiants are luminous enough to 
not be affected by systematics caused by photometric binarity. Because the 
subgiant phase is relatively short and the main-sequence lifetime has a 
strong dependence on mass, we only expect very nearly equal mass binaries to 
evolve simultaneously on the subgiant branch. Otherwise, the evolved primary 
will dominate the luminosity of the system.

At even higher luminosities---2.2---4.5 mag above the main sequence---lie what
we call the luminous subgiants. The maximum luminosity of the sample was
selected to separate it from the red clump. The extremely high luminosities of 
these targets imply that they must be descended from high-mass stars more 
massive than the Kraft break. This sample should yield useful insights in the
distribution of angular momentum as stars evolve through subgiant branch.

All other stars are considered to be giants. We don't examine them as part of
this work. Previous work have examined rapid rotation in the APOKASC giant
sample \citep{Tayar15}.

\section{Data Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
The analysis performed in this manuscript relies heavily on
measurements of rotation period, stellar radius, and \vsini. As a result, we

\subsection{Rotation Periods}
\label{sec:periodval}

One of the quality checks that \citet{McQuillan14} performs is to check their
rotation period measurements against other reported rotation periods published
in the literature. \citet{McQuillan14} compares their rotation periods to the
studies of \citet{Reinhold13} and \citet{Nielsen13}, both of which are
Fourier-based methods acting on a subset of the light curve. \citet{Reinhold13}
selected on highly-active stars using only Q3 data, and performed a generalized
Lomb-Scargle periodogram \citep{Zechmeister09}. Meanwhile \citet{Nielsen13}
ran a Lomb-Scargle periodogram with strict quality determinations based on the
noise and period range. The two methods detected 24,124 and 12,151 stars,
respectively.

When comparing periods to the previous methods, \citet{McQuillan14} find that
13,035 out of 20,009 (65\%) overlap stars agree with \citet{Reinhold13} and 
9,757 out of 10,381 (94\%) overlap stars agree with agree with
\citet{Nielsen13}. For an additional 1304 (7\%) and 202 (2\%) cases, for
\citet{Reinhold13} and \citet{Nielsen13} respectively, does \citet{McQuillan14}
claim that the alternative methods found half-period aliases based on visual
inspection of the light curve.

A more rigorous evaluation of the quality of the ACF method was done in
\citet{Aigrain15}, who injected rotation signals in quiet \Kepler{} light
curves, and had tested different algorithms to recover them in a blind manner.
The \citet{McQuillan14} algorithm was complete in identifying rotation periods
in 100\% of the sample, but had 68\% reliability in identifying the true
rotation period within the given uncertainties, which is as expected for
\(1\sigma\) uncertainties.

\citet{Aigrain15} also included an analysis of the wavelet-based method as
described in \citet{Garcia14}, which was used to measure rotation periods for
the \Kepler{} asteorseismic sample. Among the artificial light curves,
\citet{Garcia14} had a detection fraction of 78\% and a 88\% reliability in
detecting the period within the quoted uncertainties. We use the overlap sample
between \citet{Garcia14} and \citep{McQuillan14} to validate these statistics 
in the asteroseismic regime. Unfortunately, because of the difficulty in 
determining the nondetections in \citet{Garcia14}, we are not able to reliably 
validate the detection fraction. However, we can measure the reliability of 
the two samples by comparing their values, as shown in \cref{fig:periodcomp}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering

    \citet{Garcia14} for 114 overlap targets. Uncertainties are taken from the

We find that the two datasets agree well within their uncertainties with each

uncertainties derived by \citet{Garcia14} are slightly overestimated compared
to a Gaussian. This isn't surprising because the uncertainties in
\citet{Garcia14} were calculated using the half-width at half maximum of the
profile in the wavelet power spectrum, which isn't expected to correspond to a
\(1-\sigma\) interval.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

With an understanding of the quality of the data, we now use it to compare
the two measurements of rotation. Performing this analysis requires (1)
converting the rotation periods into equatorial velocity (2) convolving the
rotation velocity with a \(\sin i\) distribution and (3) incorporating
measurement error into the predicted \vsini{} distribution before evaluating
the quality of the fits. We discuss those steps in the following sections.

\subsection{Equatorial Velocity}
\label{sec:veq}

In order to convert the rotation period to velocity space, we make use of the
relation:

\begin{equation}
    \log v_{eq} = \log 2 \pi R - \log P
    \label{eq:veq}
\end{equation}

where \(R\) is the stellar radius and \(P\) is the rotation period. We perform
the transformation in log-space because our data analysis indicates that the
uncertainties in stellar radius and rotation period are fractional in nature.
Hence the propagation of the errors is more appropriate in log-space than
linear space.

Due to the temperature-dependent nature of the stellar radius, any flat cut in
period or \vsini{} space will necessarily be temperature dependent in the
transformed space. We characterized this dependence by fitting a second-order
polynomial to \citet{Huber14} radii for \citet{Huber14} dwarfs with \(4250
\textrm{ K } \le \Teff \le 5450\). Determination of a star as a \citet{Huber14}
dwarf is done by a flat cut at \(\logg > 4.2\). The hottest part of our sample
was excluded from the fit due to the strong dependence of the radius on age 
evolution; the coolest part of the sample was excluded due to poor model fits
in APOGEE spectra. The observed spread in radii is driven by inferred metallicity
differences in the models. \gvs{Add figure on this. And maybe it would be worth
doing the lower envelope instead of the median.}

In addition to fitting to the \citet{Huber14} radii, we also determine a

\subsection{Inclination}
\label{sec:inclination}

In order to transform a given equatorial velocity to a predicted
\vsini{} distribution, it is necessary to convolve with a \(\sin i\)
distribution. This distribution can be characterized as having inclinations
uniform in \(\cos i\). We calculate the predicted \vsini{} by taking the sum:

\begin{equation}
    \log \vsini = \log v_{eq} + \log \sin i
    \label{eq:vsinisum}
\end{equation}

The reason why we write the \vsini{} product in this form is because the
uncertainties of \(v_{eq}\) are Gaussian in the logarithm. The \(\log \sin i\)
term follows the distribution:

\begin{equation}
    p(\log \sin i) d(\log \sin i) = \frac{10^{2 \log \sin i}}{\sqrt{1 - 10^{2
    \log \sin i}}} d(\log \sin i)
    \label{eq:logsini}
\end{equation}

Hence, the distribution of \(\log \vsini\) can be calculated using the 
relatively simple rules involving the addition of two random variables. This
involves the convolution:

\begin{equation}
    p(\log \vsini) = \int_{-\infty}{\infty} \frac{1}{2 \pi \sigma^2} 
    e^{-\frac{(\log v_{eq} - \log v_0)^2}{2 \sigma^2}} \frac{10^{2(
    \log \vsini - \log v_{eq})}}{\sqrt{1 - 10^{2(
    \log \vsini - \log v_{eq})}}} d(\log v_{eq}) 
    \label{eq:logvsini}
\end{equation}

where \(\sigma\) is the width of gaussian, given by adding the fractional
uncertainties of the period and radius in quadrature, and \(v_0\) is the mean
value given by \(2\pi R / P\). This integral is intensive to perform for
all of the objects in our sample. Instead we elect to manually convolve the
vsini distribution on a grid. The process for the convolution is shown in
\cref{fig:convolve}. First, the distribution of \(v_{eq}\) is described as a
Gaussian. Afterward, that Gaussian is convolved with a kernel in the form of
\(\int p(\log \vsini) d(\log vsini)\) to yield a distribution of
\(\log \vsini\). The \(\log \vsini\) distribution is then convolved with a
Gaussian corresponding to the observational uncertainty to bring it in line
with what would be the observed \vsini{} distribution for a given point.

In order to get the full \vsini{} distribution for a sample, an average
probability distribution of \(v_eq\) is calculated by averaging the
distributions of all objects in the sample. The full distribution is then
convolved with \(\log \sin i\) and the uncertainty to yield the observed
\vsini{} distribution.

In order to test whether the predicted and observed distributions are
compatible, we perform a KS-test and report on the p-value between the two. If
the p-value is high, we conclude that the two measures of rotation are
consistent with each other. If the p-value is low, we conclude that the two
measures of rotation are not consistent and are likely affected by some
systematic uncertainty.

\section{Results}

In order to validate the concordance between rotational period and
\vsini{}, we perform the check described in Section~\ref{sec:astero} for the
different regimes in the HR diagram. The reason we break up the analysis into
separate regimes is that there are different radius determination methods which
are appropriate for different targets. Direct application of the
Stefan-Boltzmann law is generally the best way to derive stellar radii.
However, for stars near the main sequence, the contribution of photometric
binaries need to be corrected for. On the unevolved main sequence, this can be
done straightforwardly because all isochrones at reasonable ages yield the same
deprojection. However, in the hot dwarf domain, there is a degeneracy between
enhanced luminosity due to age and due to the presence of photometric binaries.

The strategy that we take is to make use of isochrone radii on the lower main
sequence, where age effects are minimal, to use the Stefan-Boltzmann law for
subgiants, where binarity is minimal, and to treat the hot dwarf regime
carefully. For hot unevolved stars, where there is no significant contribution
from a binary, we measure the radius using the Stefan-Boltzmann law, but for
hot evolved stars, we quantify the rate at which the two measurements disagree
to understand the photometric binary fraction in that regime.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

By comparing the agreement between the spectroscopic and photometric measures
of rotation throughout the HR diagram, we have found that there is structure in
whether these measurements agree or not. The two measures appear to agree well
for the asteroseismic sample, the photometric singles in the hot dwarf regime,
and for the subgiants and luminous subgiants. For the cool dwarfs and the
photometric binaries in the hot dwarf regime, the \vsini{} measurements are
broader than predicted by the rotation periods. We conclude that in the cool 
and hot dwarfs, there persists a systematic in any of the radius, \vsini{} or 
rotation period, and we consider each of those in turn. 

If the systematic was in the radius, this would mean that the equatorial
velocity, hence the radius was underestimated by factors of several. The radius
is determined using just two quantities: the luminosity and temperature. It's
highly unlikely that the temperatures are systematically overestimated by tthat
much because the temperatures were calibrated to the infrared-flux scale
\citep{GonzalezHernandez09} and to clusters \citep{Holtzman18}. If the
effective temperatures are accurate, then any radius problems should lie in the
luminosities being underestimated. So far, there is no credible mechanism
behind why the luminosities should be estimated. Any residual effect of
photometric binaries should be to overestimate the luminosities rather than
underestimate them \citep{ElBadry18a, Simonian19}. Additionally, the problem is
less severe in the hot dwarfs, where we did not correct the luminosity for
photometric binarity.

A systematic in the period would require that the period was overestimated.
There are a number of plausible ways in which this could happen in cases with
photometric binaries. One is that in cases where both binaries are spotted and
have similar rotation rates, the ACF algorithm of \citet{McQuillan14} may
detect the beat frequency of the composite signal rather than the rotation
frequency. \gvs{Is this plausible with \Kepler{}'s high cadence?} This can 
explain why the spectroscopically-derived rotation rate is much higher than the
photometrically-derived rotation rate. In addition to the the photometric
period being overestimated, this mechanism would also need to explain why there
are spectroscopically broadened stars with no photometric period at all. This
may be plausible if the additional light of the secondary diluted the
photometric period signal below the detection threshold of the ACF. \gvs{I can
    check to see if there's a period which didn't meet their detectability
threshold that is consistent with the equatorial velocity.} A more detailed
analysis using the rotation-activity-age relationship can shed light on how
blended rotation signals affect the detectability of rotation.

Our favored explanation for this discrepancy is that the \vsini{} are
overestimated. Hints of this were shown in the Pleiades comparison, where the
APOGEE \vsini{} preferentially disagreed with higher-resolution spectroscopy
for photometric binaries. We interpret this behavior as the APOGEE spectrograph
misclassifying unresolved binaries as rapid rotators. This would only occur for
binary systems for which the RV offset between the components \(\Delta v =
\textrm{c}/\mathcal{R} \sim 14 \kms\) is comparable to the velocity resolution
of the spectrograph. This mechanism would predict that there would be a
sharp transition in \vsini{} between this contamination of rapid rotators and
stars labeled as resolved spectroscopic binaries. As is evident from
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, there is a transition \vsini{} between
these inconsistent rapid rotators and the double-lined spectroscopic binaries.
This hypothesis can be tested with high-resolution spectroscopic follow-up of
these broad-lined targets to check if they decompose into two narrow components.
This type of contamination is most prevalent in the old \Kepler{} population
because true broad-lined stars are intrinsically rare. In young stellar
populations, such as the Pleiades, the rotational broadening of most stars is
already on the order of the instrumental resolution, hence the discrepancy
between the photometric and spectroscopic rotation measures would be much
smaller in these cases.

One conclusion we make is that APOGEE \vsini{} measured near the main-sequence 
can have spurious rapid rotators due to the presence of photometric binaries 
with radial velocity offsets. We also take the lesson that the APOGEE
\vsini{} can be used to reliably measure rotation in regimes away from the main
sequence. In fact, spectroscopic rotation is currently the best measure of
rotation in the subgiant domain, which does not have a uniformly-analyzed
photometric rotational dataset.

\subsection{Subgiant Rotation}

Knowing that the APOGEE \vsini{} yield appropriate results in the subgiant
regime, we can use the \vsini{} to validate broad trends in rotation predicted
by stellar angular momentum models \citep{vanSaders13}. It's observed that
massive stars above the Kraft break rotate extremely quickly on the main
sequence \citep{Kraft67,Abt95}. As these stars evolve onto the subgiant branch, 
one of the broad-stroke predictions of angular momentum evolution models are 
that rapid rotation can actually persist quite far along the subgiant branch.
In fact, at fixed \Teff{}, stars of higher mass are expected to have larger
rotation rates (see Fig. 2 in \citet{vanSaders13}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared the rotation periods and spectroscopic \vsini{} for a sample of
\gvs{Double-check num} dwarfs and subgiants observed by APOGEE.\@ By comparing
the \vsini{} to previously observed dwarfs in the Pleiades with high-resolution
spectroscopy, we calibrate the APOGEE \vsini{} and determine a detection 
threshold of 10 \kms and fractional uncertainty of around 10\%. We also find 
discrepancies between the APOGEE \vsini{} and literature \vsini{} for 
\gvs{NOTE FRACTION} of photometric binaries.

We also find a strong failure mode in samples that contain photometric
binaries. This can occur either due to complex variation from two superimposed
signals causing rotational periods to be overestimated, or it can be due to
intermediate-period photometric binaries in an orbital configuration where
their RV difference is on the order of the APOGEE velocity resolution. We favor
the latter explanation because it is consistent with the \vsini{} discrepancies
we noticed in the Pleiades. 

For stars away from the main sequence, the APOGEE \vsini{} provides a valuable
resource in understanding the rotational evolution of evolved stars. We compare
the measured \vsini{} to values predicted by the models in \citet{vanSaders13}.
We find that the data are generally in agreement with the model.
