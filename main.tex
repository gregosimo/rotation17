\begin{abstract}
  The \Kepler{} satellite enabled measurements of 34,030 of
  stellar rotation periods through starspot variability. We use over 5000
  spectroscopic \vsini{} measurements of \Kepler{} dwarfs and
  subgiants from the APOGEE survey as an independent measures of rotation. 
  Our data confirms the reliability of the extension of the \Kepler{} Input
  Catalog using 2MASS \Teff{} estimates in \citet{Huber14}. We use \Gaia{} 
  parallaxes and APOGEE temperatures to calculate radii 
  to transform between these two measures of rotation. We find a much higher
  occurrence rate of spectroscopic rapid rotators among photometric binaries
  than is seen in the photometric rotation period sample. We attribute this
  difference to unresolved spectroscopic binaries with velocity displacements 
  on the order of the APOGEE resolution, which can lead to a spurious
  overestimate of rotational broadening. This binary background can be dominant for intrinsically
  slowly-rotating populations. For the subgiants, we use asteroseismic data to
  demonstrate that period and \vsini{} methods agree, indicating the \vsini{}
  measurements are less impacted by the presence of binaries for these targets. 
  We see clear evidence for a transition between rapid and slow rotation on the
  subgiant branch in the domain predicted by angular momentum evolution models.
  We discuss future tests quantifying the impact of binarity on rotation
  measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rotation is a fundamental property of stars. Stars are born with a wide range 
of rotation rates as they leave the pre-main sequence \citep{Attridge92,
Herbst00, Henderson12}. Once on the main sequence, \citet{Kraft67} noticed a
dichotomy in rotation rates, with higher-mass stars maintaining a wide range of
rotation rates, while cooler stars rotate substantially more slowly. The
transition between the two is thought to be because of the onset of convective
envelopes in lower-mass stars, which lose angular momentum through magnetized
winds \citep{Parker58,Weber67,Kawaler88}. 

Rotation can be measured in two different ways. One method measures
the projected broadening of spectral lines due to rotation \citep{Kahler89},
which requires intensive high-resolution spectroscopy.  The sensitivity of 
this measurement also depends strongly on the rotation rate leading to data 
truncation effects. An additional method
which has been expanded greatly by the \Kepler{} satellite is the measurement
of rotation periods through modulations in the light curves of starspots
\citep{Basri11,Affer12,Nielsen13,Reinhold13,McQuillan14,Garcia14}.
These measurements take advantage of long stretches of continuous
coverage in space to have uniform sensitivity over the length of the
observation. Unlike the spectral lines, rotational periods are sensitive to
much slower rotation rates because their limit is determined by the length of
the observations rather than the resolution of the spectrograph.
However, they can only be measured for stars with spots, which depends
sensitively on the strength of surface magnetic fields, and can be subject to 
systematic effects such as differential rotation and variability 
\citep{Epstein14}. 

Rotation is valuable for understanding stellar physics not just because it can
tell us about stellar dynamos and winds, but also because rotation may yield 
inferences of stellar ages, a
technique known as gyrochronology \citep{Barnes07,Mamajek08}. By
studying the evolution of rotation in open clusters, gyrochronology relations
have been derived to infer ages from rotation rates and masses. However, open
cluster rotation measurements are biased toward young ages, and there are few 
old calibrators available. Attempts to calibrate the relations using \Kepler{} stars with 
asteroseismic ages revealed challenges in extending existing gyrochronology
relationships \citep{Angus15,vanSaders16}.

While there have been several attempts to evaluate the rotation
periods of \Kepler{} stars, they have all been through
comparisons between different periodogram analysis, but not with independent 
measures of rotation, such as \vsini. The advent of large spectroscopic
surveys, such as the Apache Point Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE), has
enabled the measurement of \vsini{} for hundreds of thousands of stars.
However, APOGEE measures \vsini{} by performing a \(\chi^2\) minimization on
the observed spectra rather than the traditional cross-correlation routine to
measure line broadening \citep{Gray08}. Using the
large overlap between \Kepler{} rotation periods and APOGEE \vsini{}, we can 
evaluate the quality of each of these measurements of rotation.

Most work on stellar rotation has focused on unevolved main-sequence stars. The
large number of rotation periods/\vsini{} and the wide availability of
\Gaia{} parallaxes facilitates the study of rotation off the main sequence, such
as for subgiants.  Subgiants are rare in young clusters, which are the main
calibrators for understanding stellar rotation, and they have been difficult 
to distinguish from unevolved stars in
the field due to poor luminosity estimates. Parallax measurements from the 
\Gaia{} satellite changed this landscape substantially, enabling precise
dwarf/subgiant discrimination in the field. 

In this paper, we compare the rotation distribution of \Kepler{} targets to
that seen from APOGEE \vsini{}s. Section~\ref{sec:sample} describes the data 
necessary to perform the analysis in this work. We validate the uncertainties 
and detection thresholds of \vsini{} and 2MASS-based temperatures in 
Section~\ref{sec:validation}. We discuss our methodology for comparing the 
rotation and \vsini{} in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, and present the
comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:rotcomp}. We then show the distribution of
rapid rotators in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Lastly, we discuss potential reasons for
inconsistencies and compare to models in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. 

\section{Catalog Data}
\label{sec:sample}

The base sample for our study of rotation in the \Kepler{} field is the 
sample of \Kepler{} stars observed by APOGEE. The selection
function for this sample is described in \citet{Zasowski17}. The majority of
targets were chosen for asteroseismic studies of evolved red giants, but \(\sim
8,000\) unevolved stars were targeted by the survey. The APOGEE spectra provide stellar
parameters, as well as rotational broadenings. There is a signficant overlap
between the APOGEE sample and the rotation period sample of
\citet{McQuillan14}. As a
result, the APOKASC sample has two independent measures of rotation:
photometric rotation periods and projected rotational velocities. To compare
these two measures statistically, we estimate stellar radii, from photometry
and astronometry.  Fortunately, the \Gaia{} DR2 parallaxes provide excellent 
distance measurements for our targets, and extinction is also well studied in 
these fields. The details of the input catalogs are described in more detail 
below. 

\subsection{APOGEE}
\label{sec:APOGEE}

The underlying sample is drawn from the Data Release 14 \citep{Abolfathi18}
of the APOGEE survey \citep{Majewski17}. The workhorse behind the survey is the
APOGEE spectrograph, a high-resolution (\(R \sim 22,000\)) multi-fiber
near-infrared spectrograph \citep{Wilson10} mounted on the SDSS 2.5-meter
telescope at Apache Point Observatory \citep{Gunn06}.

Reduction of the APOGEE data takes place in three main stages. First,
individual visit spectra are reduced, including detector calibration, bad pixel
masking, wavelength calibration, sky subtraction, and determination of
individual radial velocities through cross-correlation of template spectra.
Second, the individual spectra are combined by correcting for radial velocity
differences between the exposures, either by cross-correlating with each other,
or with template spectra, whichever leads to a smaller scatter
\citep{Holtzman18}. The full process is detailed in \citet{Nidever15}.

The final step is the extraction of stellar parameters and chemical abundances
by the APOGEE Stellar Parameter and Chemical Abundances Pipeline (ASPCAP)
\citep{GarciaPerez16}. ASPCAP measures stellar parameters by performing a
chi-squared minimization \citep{AllendePrieto06} over a grid of stellar
atmospheres in three runs. Stellar atmospheres are a combination of ATLAS9 
and MARCS model
atmospheres \citep{Meszaros12,Zamora15} which are split into multiple grids
according to temperature and evolutionary state: GK giants, GK dwarfs, M
giants, M dwarfs, and F giants.  A coarse run determines the grid to be used 
by holding abundance to be solar and selecting the grid with the
lowest \(\chi^2\) value. For dwarf grids, the second run is more refined with a 
6-dimensional search in the full space of \Teff, \logg, [M/H], [\(\alpha\)/M], 
\vsini, and microturbulent velocity. Once
those six parameters have been determined, ASPCAP then measures abundances for
individual elements, including \feh{}. In the case of giants, \vsini{} is fixed
to zero, while the carbon and nitrogen abundances are allowed to be free
parameters.

After determination of the stellar parameters and chemical abundances, the 
effective temperatures are calibrated to the photometric system of 
\citet{GonzalezHernandez09} using a metallicity-dependent offset, and the 
abundances are calibrated to provide homogeneous results within clusters. 
After calibration, the scatter between the 
calibrated and photometric temperatures  were found to be a function of 
\Teff{}, ranging from 200 K at 7000 K down to 85 K at 4000 K
\citep{Holtzman18}. The statistical uncertainty in \feh{} was measured to be 
0.009 dex \citep{Holtzman18}, but comparisons to other high-resolution spectra
indicates that the true random error is closer to 0.1 dex \citep{Serenelli17}.

The APOGEE \vsini{} measurements just became included as catalog values in
DR14 after initially being included as a nuisance broadening parameter.
As a result, they have not been thoroughly validated. \citet{Tayar15} 
measured \vsini{} directly from the ASPCAP spectra and validated them for
evolved stars. We perform a validation of the catalog \vsini{} for main-sequene
stars (see Section~\ref{sec:vsinival}).

One of the driving efforts for \Kepler{} field observations has been
the spectroscopic characterization of asteroseismic targets 
\citep{Zasowski17,Pinsonneault18}; 11,734 targets are part of that sample. The 
asteroseismic sample is substantially biased toward giants and subgiants due 
to their high amplitude oscillations. The next largest program complements 
the asteroseismic program by observing the remaining portion of the \Kepler{} 
sample cooler than 6500 K and brighter than \(H < 11\), an additional 3193 
targets. An additional 
695 targets were part of an effort to characterize \Kepler{} planet hosts with 
\(H < 14\), and the remaining 102 targets were targeted to follow-up eclipsing 
binaries \citep{Prsa11,Slawson11}.  The full APOGEE sample is shown in the left-hand panel of
\cref{fig:regimes}. These broad categories are illustrated in
\cref{fig:targeting}.

We inspected the fits to a set of spectra representative of targets flagged 
by the ASPCAP pipeline as having potential quality problems. A small fraction
(2.6\%) of targets have the \STARBAD{} quality flag enabled. This quality flag
is triggered when any of the model parameters hit a grid edge, the chi-square
of the fit is poor, the spectrum has poor signal to noise, or if the
color temperature of the targets are substantially different from the
spectroscopic temperature. Visual inspection of a representative sample of 
spectra indicates that the flag is largely the result of poor subtraction or 
normalization of the spectrum in the pipeline, or due to poor model fits on the 
cool (\(\Teff < 4250 K\)) end. However, there is a small fraction of objects
where the \STARBAD{} flag was only triggered because the \vsini{} hit a grid
edge. We manually calibrate stellar parameters for these stars 
\citep{Holtzman18}, and treat those few targets as \vsini{} lower limits. The
remaining \STARBAD{} objects are excluded from the sample. We did not remove
targets with the \STARWARN{} flag because visual inspection of a representative
sample of these spectra indicates that the fits reasonably resemble the
underlying spectra. We include all targets with the \STARWARN{} 
quality flag enabled.

Because we are interested in \vsini{} for this analysis, one potentially
significant class of contaminants are double-lined spectroscopic binaries
(SB2s). The ASPCAP pipeline intrinsically assumes a single-star stellar 
atmosphere. If there are double-lines in the spectrum, it will attempt to fit 
an extremely broad single line profile on top of both lines, resulting in a 
spurious rapid rotator. In order to exclude these targets from the analysis, 
we visually inspected the spectra of all targets near the main sequence with 
\vsini{} greater than \(10 \kms\) (the detection threshold determined in
Section~\ref{sec:vsinival}). If a given star showed signs of double-lined spectra
either in the combined spectrum, or in any of the individual visit spectra, it
was flagged as a SB2. An illustrative example of each case is shown in 
\cref{fig:sb2diag}.

\subsubsection{Binarity}

A portion of the APOGEE sample has binarity measurements from
\citet{ElBadry18b}. \citet{ElBadry18b} ran a machine-learning algorithm to flag
stars which had spectra that were better fit with a binary model than with a 
single star model. It was run on the DR13 release, which has substantially
fewer dwarfs and subgiants than the DR15 release. As a result, only 17\% of our
sample was analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b}.

For stars with poor single-star fits, \citet{ElBadry18b} attempted to
find a better-fitting model. Additional models that they considered were: SB1,
SB2, SB2 with hidden triple component, and SB3. For the best-fit model,
\citet{ElBadry18b} fitted a wider set of parameters for each component. 
While this method yields extremely precise estimates, determining the true 
errors in these parameters is extremely difficult. As a result, we forgo 
using the revised quantities, and instead use the binarity flags in 
\citet{ElBadry18b} to distinguish single stars from photometric binaries. We 
label stars in the classes ``Single'' and ``SB1'' as
being photometric singles, because there is very little photometric
contribution from the companion. The classes ``SB2'', ``hidden triple'' and
``SB3'' are considered photometric binaries because there is a measurable
contribution of light from the secondary.


\subsection{Rotation Periods}
\label{sec:periods}

The main sample of rotation periods in this work comes from
\citet{McQuillan14}. The primary selection criteria for targets to be analyzed
in \citet{McQuillan14} was to distinguish between dwarfs and giants by using
the \Teff-\logg{} cuts advocated by \citet{Ciardi11}. In order to avoid 
confusion with other periodic signals, \citet{McQuillan14} removed known 
eclipsing binaries \citep{Prsa11,Slawson11} and KOIs \citep{Akeson13}. They 
also imposed a \Teff{} limit of less than 6500 K to reduce confusion with 
pulsators. Their final sample to be analyzed contained 133,030 stars

\citet{McQuillan14} calculated rotation periods using an autocorrelation
function (ACF) based method. Once the first period peak was identified in the
ACF, they evaluated the significance of the peak using two main criteria: that 
the peak was persistent over most of the \Kepler{} light curve, and that the 
amplitude of the peak was larger than a \Teff{} and period-dependent threshold 
selected to compromise between real detections and false positives. The result 
was a list of 34,030 stars with period detections, as well as a list of 
99,000 stars with no significant period detection.

In addition to the \citet{McQuillan14} periods, we also make use of surface 
rotation periods derived for a sample of 310 \Kepler{} stars showing
asteroseismic oscillations \citep{Garcia14}. Unlike the methodology of
\citet{McQuillan14}, which was based on the autocorrelation of light curves,
the analysis in \citet{Garcia14} is based on wavelet analysis. We use 
\citet{Garcia14} data as a basis dataset for the APOKASC asteroseismic sample 
for which they were calculated. The asteroseismic sample with rotation periods
in \citet{Garcia14} is shown in the left-hand panel of \cref{fig:regimes}.

\subsection{Astrometry}
\label{sec:astrometry}

The availability of high-quality parallaxes from the \Gaia{} mission's Data
Release 2 \citep{Gaia18} enables both the determination of
radii for the APOGEE sample, as well as evolutionary state classifications for
stars. The \Gaia{} DR2 performed a fully consistent single-star
5-parameter (\(\alpha\), \(\delta\), \(\mu_\alpha\), \(\mu_\delta\), \(\pi\))
solution to 1.3 billion sources over 22 months of observations
\citep{Lindegren18}. For targets which could not be adequately 
fit with a single-star 5-parameter solution, a 2-parameter (\(\alpha\), \(\delta\))
solution is performed instead \citep{Michalik15}. The criteria for a
2-parameter fall-back solution include targets with \Gaia{} \(G > 21.0\) mag, 
having fewer than 6 visibility periods of observations, and an error ellipse 
larger than a magnitude-dependent limit in its largest dimension 
\citep{Lindegren18}.

We use the cross-matched database of \citet{Berger18b} to match \Kepler{} targets
against \Gaia{} DR2 detections.  \citet{Berger18b} cross-matched targets in 
the KSPC DR25 \citep{Mathur17} with \Gaia{} DR2 sources using both position 
and expected \(G\)-band flux. Of the successfully cross-matched targets, 
\citet{Berger18b} excluded objects with fractional parallax errors greater 
than 0.2, \(\Teff < 3000\) K, \(\logg < 0.1\), and low quality 2MASS 
photometry. Of the 16,915 objects observed by DR14, 778 did not make it in the
catalog by \citet{Berger18b}. Of the 778, only 273 are consistent with not
being giants according to the classification in \citet{Holtzman18}. 46 of 
these objects have no counterpart in \Gaia{} DR2. The rest were excluded by 
the \citet{Berger18b} quality cuts.  Because the targets in \citet{Berger18b} 
have high-quality parallaxes, we use the traditional formula for deriving 
distance modulus from parallax instead of the Bayesian method advocated by 
\citet{Luri18} to reduce computational complexity. We include a zero-point 
offset of 50.2 \(\mu\)as determined from comparisons to asteroseismic radii 
\citep{Zinn18}.

\subsection{Photometry}
\label{sec:photometry}

In order to characterize our stellar sample, we choose to use
\Ks-band photometry to infer bolometric luminosity, which minimizes the impact 
of extinction. \Ks-band apparent magnitudes were measured by the 2MASS survey \citep{Skrutskie06}, 
which uniformly performed profile-fit photometry and derived 
uncertainties for the full \Kepler{} field.  The typical \Ks-band 
photometric uncertainty for the APOGEE sample is 0.017 mag. A very small 
fraction of targets (0.3\%) of the 
APOGEE sample have 2MASS photometry flagged due to blending. In order to 
ensure that all the photometry in our sample is uniformly analyzed, we 
excluded these blended objects. 

\subsection{Extinction}
\label{sec:extinction}

We use estimates of extinction from the \citet{Berger18b} catalog. They
interpolated reddening vectors of \(A_V\) based on the 3-D reddening map of
\citet{Green18}. The median extinction (with \(1\sigma\) quartile ranges) for
the full sample is \(0.18^{+0.16}_{-0.08}\). However, the high range of the 
extinction is driven by the giants, which are not the target of interest for 
our study. For the sample of non-giants as described in 
Section~\ref{sec:regimes}, the distribution of extinction is 
\(0.12^{+0.12}_{-0.04}\), substantially more compact. Applying the

\subsection{Photometric Temperature}

For stars which do not have APOGEE spectroscopic temperatures, we use the
\citet{Berger18b} photometric temperatures, which are available for nearly the
entire \Kepler{} sample. The \citet{Berger18b} temperatures are updated versions
of the temperatures determined by the Kepler Stellar Parameter Catalog 
\citep{Mathur17}, who used the methodology in \citet{Huber14} to fit stellar 
parameters to sets of DSEP evolutionary tracks \citep{Dotter16}. 

The input values to the fits are include previously-determined effective
temperatures. The previously-determined effective temperature chosen follows a
prioritization scheme, with spectroscopic temperatures being used where
available, then followed by photometric temperatures. The temperature, \logg,
metallicity, distance, and extinction are then solved simultaneously.
\citet{Berger18b} used updated temperatures over \citet{Mathur17} from the 
California Kepler Survey \citep{Petigura17} and from \citep{Gaidos16}.

\section{Data Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Before comparing the APOGEE \vsini{} to the \Kepler{} rotation periods, we
first need to characterize the intrinsic uncertainties of the rotation periods,
\vsini{} and stellar radii. The rotation periods in \Kepler{} have extensive 
cross-comparisons and the agreement in general is quite good for rapid rotators
like those investigated in this work, with autocorrelation function and 
wavelet methods performing best. \citet{Aigrain15}
injected rotation signals into quiescent \Kepler{} light curves and based upon
a blinded search found that the \citet{McQuillan14} algorithm identified 
rotation periods in 100\% of the samples which had them, and had an 80\% 
reliability in identifying the true rotation period within the given 
uncertainties. The analysis with the \citet{Garcia14} method was not blinded,
but yielded a detection fraction of 78\% and an 88\% reliability; however,
rotation periods based on the \citet{Garcia14} algorithm are not yet available
for the full \Kepler{} field.

Use of the APOGEE \vsini{} in the dwarf and subgiant regime is relatively 
novel, and so we dedicate a more
detailed analysis to characterize those measurements, along with potential
impacts of binarity on these observations. We compare APOGEE
\vsini{} to literature values from high-resolution spectra in order to
determine the detection threshold and uncertainty of the \vsini{}. 

Finally, we compare the APOGEE \Teff{} to the photometric temperatures derived
by \citet{Huber14} using only 2MASS photometry for stars with saturated KIC
photometry to quantify the reliability of the 2MASS photometry in both
the dwarf/subgiant and the giant domains.

\subsection{\vsini{} Validation}
\label{sec:vsinival}

APOGEE \vsini{} measurements have been used and validated for giants 
\citep{Tayar15}, but not for dwarfs. \citet{Tayar15} derived \vsini{} by
cross-correlating APOGEE spectra, and found good agreement with
\vsini{} values from high-resolution spectroscopy of subgiants, with a robust
detection threshold of \(7 \kms\), and an uncertainty of 10\%.

sharp-lined objects, and it is easier to measure broadening in them than in
dwarf stars with larger line broadening. We choose this

investigated by \citet{Tayar15}. 

We determined the APOGEE uncertainty by modeling the literature and APOGEE
observations as independent observations with lognormally-distributed errors
for the \citet{Stauffer87}, \citet{Queloz98}, and \citet{Jackson18} samples. We
choose those three samples because there is a large overlap with APOGEE, 
unlike with the \citet{Stauffer84} and \citet{Terndrup00} samples, and 
because they report uncertainties, unlike \citet{Soderblom93}.
We used an MCMC routine \citep{ForemanMackey13} with a likelihood function:
\begin{equation}
    L(\{v_{1,i}\}, \{v_{2,i}\} | \{\sigma_{1,i}\}, \sigma_2, \{v_{i}\}) =
    \prod_{i} \mathcal{N}(\log v_{1,i} ; \log v_i, \sigma_{1,i}) \mathcal{N}(
    \log v_{2,i}; \log v_i, \sigma_2)
\end{equation}
where \(\{v_{1,i}\}, \{v_{2,i}\}\) and \(\sigma_{1,i}\) are the known
    literature \vsini{}, APOGEE \vsini{}, and literature \vsini{} error, 
    \(\{v_i\}\) are the unobserved true vsini, and \(\sigma_2\) is the
    uncertainty we are trying to derive. We adopt flat priors for all
    \(\{v_i\}\) and \(\sigma_2\) between \(\log_{10}(1)\) and 
    \(\log_{10}(100)\). As observed in \cref{fig:vsinicomp}, the APOGEE \vsini{} 
    for photometric binaries is occasionally substantially different than 
    that from the literature. We discuss the potential impact of photometric 
    binaries in Section~\ref{sec:binaries}, and exclude them from the 
    following analysis.

\subsection{Photometric Binaries and \vsini{}}
\label{sec:binaries}

A major potential source of systematic uncertainty for both stellar radius and
\vsini{} is the presence of a luminous, blended companion (i.e.\ a
photometric binary). As is seen in \cref{fig:vsinicomp}, single stars in the
Pleiades have concordant \vsini{}, while some photometric binaries do not. Our
stellar radius estimates can be biased by the presence of a companion because
the implicit assumption of the luminosity being given off from the surface of a
mono-temperature sphere in the Stefan-Boltzmann law would be violated, and
radii would be overestimated. Additionally, the presence of a companion in the
spectrum can also cause the temperature to be overestimated on average
\citep{ElBadry18a}, and individual temperatures to be scattered in large and
unpredictable ways \citep{Simonian19}. \vsini{} can also be affected by blends 
of spectral lines by luminous companions. In this section, we focus on 
quantifying the rate of photometric binaries which could potentially affect 
stellar parameters in this sample.

The most reliable method to determine the presence of a luminous companion in
the Pleiades is to select photometric binaries. We consider objects in the
Pleiades to be photometric binaries if they lie greater than 0.3 mag above the
main-sequence locus in \(\Delta V\) vs \(V-K\) as measured by 
\citet{Stauffer16}. The photometric binaries for the full sample are shown in
the top panel of \cref{fig:vsinicomp}, and consist of 21\% of the Pleiades
\vsini{} sample. 

We estimate the rate of \vsini{} inconsistencies among the binaries by 
calculating a \(\chi^2\) figure of merit between the
literature and APOGEE \vsini{} values. If the \vsini{} values are more than
\(3\sigma\) discrepant, then we flag them as inconsistent. If the same star was
measured in multiple datasets, we classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as consistent,
if the \vsini{} measurement was within \(3\sigma\) for all datasets, we
classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as inconsistent if it is inconsistent at the
\(3\sigma\) level for all datasets, and classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as
ambiguous if it is consistent with some datasets and inconsistent with others.
We don't include measurements from \citep{Soderblom93} because they did not
include uncertainties in their \vsini{} measurements. In our sample, we have 
24 photometric binaries with \vsini{} detections in APOGEE and in at least 
one literature source. Of them, 17 are consistent, 3 are inconsistent, and 4
are ambiguous. Depending on whether we classify the ambiguous cases as
inconsistencies with APOGEE, or inconsistencies with other datasets, we 
estimate that between 12--30\% of photometric binaries have \vsini{} measured
in APOGEE that is inconsistent with that found in the literature.


For more massive stars where age effects are important, we expect the impact of
binarity to be reduced due to the increased luminosity of the primary. Because
the impact of binarity is degenerate with that of age in the hot star domain, 
we use the classification of \citet{ElBadry18b} as an independent measure of the presence 
of a luminous companion.  In the cool dwarf regime, where photometric binaries can be
easily separated from photometric singles, we find that of the 421 stars
analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b}, only 56\% of photometric binaries are flagged
as SB2s, but 92\% of SB2s flagged by \citet{ElBadry18b} are photometric
binaries, implying that the \citet{ElBadry18b} algorithm reliably, but not
completely traces binarity.

To quantify the impact of binarity in the hot star regime, we compare the
binary fraction as inferred from the \citet{ElBadry18b} classification between
the cool stars, where binarity has a large impact, and the hot stars, where
the effect of binarity should be diluted. In the cool dwarf regime, we find
that 75 out of 421 stars are detected as binaries, for a photometric binary
fraction of \(17.6^{+2.3}_{-1.7}\)\%. For the combined hot dwarfs, subgiants, and
giants, 63 out of 527 stars are detected as binarites, for a photometric binary
fraction of \(11.9^{+1.6}_{-1.4}\)\%, supporting the finding that the impact of
binaries is reduced for evolved stars compared to the unevolved main sequence.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

Now with a better understanding of the systematic and statistical uncertainties
of the \vsini{} and rotation periods, we proceed to test
whether they yield consistent results. Because we are largely interested in the 
rapid rotator fraction in this work rather than the full rotation distribution,
we set the rapid rotator fraction in the photometric rotation periods and the 
spectroscopic \vsini{}s as our figure of merit. We then proceed to test the 
appropriateness of this methodology in the well-studied asteroseismic sample, 
where the stellar radius should be known to high accuracy, and get that it is 
reliable.

\subsection{Equatorial Velocity}
\label{sec:veq}

In order to convert the rotation period to velocity space, we make use of the
relation:

\begin{equation}
    \log v_{eq} = \log 2 \pi R - \log P
    \label{eq:veq}
\end{equation}
where \(R\) is the stellar radius and \(P\) is the rotation period. We perform
the transformation in log-space because the uncertainties in stellar radius 
and rotation period are fractional in nature.

\begin{figure*}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{period_vel}
    \caption{\emph{Left:} HR diagram for the APOGEE sample showing radius as
    determined through \Ks-band luminosity. Red stars indicate 
the median radius in each of the Cool Dwarf, Hot Dwarf, Subgiant, 
Luminous Subgiant, and RGB Base regimes. Color maps the distribution of 
stellar radii.  \emph{Right:} Maximum period calculated as a function of 
stellar radius with color corresponding to the stellar radius. The same stars 
with median radius are also shown in this figure, with radii of 0.77, 1.2, 
2.1, 3.1, and 4.6 solar radii.}
    \label{fig:period_vel}
\end{figure*}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:vsinival}, the reliable detection limit for
dwarf and subgiant APOGEE \vsini{}s is 10 \kms, which translates to a 
radius-dependent period threshold illustrated in \cref{fig:period_vel}. 
Our detection threshold of 10 \kms corresponds to a period cutoff of 3.7 days 
for a \(R = 0.77 R_\sun\) star, typical of the cool dwarf sample; 6.0 days for 
a \(R = 1.2 R_\sun\) hot dwarf; 10.4 days for a \(R = 2.1 R_\sun\) subgiant; 
15.5 days for a \(R = 3.1 R_\sun\) luminous subgiant; and 23.5 days for a
\(R = 4.6 R_\sun\) lower RGB star. 

To quantify the agreement between the photometric rapid rotator distribution
and the spectroscopic rapid rotator distribution, we take the observed
distribution of \(v_{eq}\) (given \(P_{rot}\) and \(R\), convolve it with a 
    \(\sin i\) distribution, and calculate which fraction of the convolved 
    distribution should remain above the
\vsini{} detection limit. Previous attempts to compare rotation periods to
\vsini{} in clusters found that starspot modulations are not detected for stars
which are inclined by \(\sin i < 0.5\), so we truncate our \(\sin i\)
convolution kernel at \(\sin i = 0.5\) \citep{Jackson10}.

\subsection{Stellar Radius}
\label{sec:GaiaR}

The integral quantity necessary to compare projected rotational velocities to
rotation periods is the stellar radius.  We calculate isochrone-independent 
radii by making use of the K-band luminosity and temperature through the 
Stefan-Boltzmann law:

\begin{equation}
    \log R = \log L_\sun - 0.4 \left(\Ks +5 \log \left(\frac{\omega}{100
    \textrm{ mas }} \right) + BC(\Teff, \feh) - M_{bol,\sun}\right) - 4 \log
        \Teff - \log 4\pi\sigma
\end{equation}
where \Ks{} is the 2MASS \Ks-band flux, \(\omega\) is the \Gaia{} parallax,
\(BC\) is the bolometric correction in the \Ks-band, \Teff{} is the effective temperature,
\feh{} is the metallicity, \(M_{bol},\sun=4.74\) is the absolute bolometric 
magnitude of the Sun \citep{Mamajek15}, and \(\sigma\) is the Stefan-Boltzmann 
constant. All of these quantities except for the bolometric correction are available in the 
combined APOGEE/\Gaia/2MASS catalogs. We write this equation as a logarithm
under the assumption that the dominant errors in this analysis, from
\vsini{} and \Teff{}, are fractional in nature.

We calculate the bolometric correction using the ATLAS12/SYNTHE 
\citet{Kurucz70, Kurucz93} stellar atmosphere models included with the MESA 
Isochrones and Stellar Tracks
\citep[MIST]{Dotter16,Choi16} version 1.2. We interpolate over \Teff{} and
\feh{} to yield bolometric corrections. To gauge the systematic uncertainty 
in the MIST bolometric correction, we compare the MIST bolometric correction 
to the bolometric corrections reported in \citet{Casagrande10} at three
different metallicities, and find that the systematic uncertainty in the
bolometric correction is about 0.01 mag, which is negligible for our purposes.
We compare our \Ks-band radius to the SED-fitted radius in \citet{Berger18b}
and find that our radii and those from \citet{Berger18b} are consistent to within the formal errors with a scatter of 
3\%, which is substantially smaller than the random errors imposed by \Teff{} 
and the potential effect of binarity.

Another major systematic uncertainty when calculating radii through the K-band
luminosity is the effect of binarity. Binary systems are not well-described as
a single-temperature spherical blackbody; as a result, the radii calculated
from them will not be physical. 

For the \Kepler{} cool dwarf sample, we derive radii by assuming that
the single star relation can be characterized by the MIST isochrones. The 
MIST stellar models are based on the Modules for Experiments in Stellar 
Astrophysics (MESA) evolutionary tracks \citep{Paxton11,Paxton13,Paxton15}.  
A few representative 1 Gyr isochrones are shown in the left panel of 
\cref{fig:regimes}.

When directly calculating radii from MIST isochrones, we interpolate on a
1 Gyr solar metallicity isochrone given an object's \Teff{}. We ignore
the metallicity of the isochrone because of a previous result that the
isochrones overpredict the dependence of the bolometric
luminosity on metallicity, and that a single-metallicity isochrone may yield
more accurate results than adjusting for the metallicity \citep{Simonian19}.

\subsection{Regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \plotone{regimes}
  \caption{The APOGEE targets subdivided into four regimes: cool 
      dwarfs (purple), hot dwarfs (mustard), subgiants (teal), and giants 
      (red). The separation between hot and cool samples is at 
      \(\Teff = 5250\) K. The separations in luminosity correspond to 
      \(\Delta \MK = -1.2 \textrm{ and } -4.75\). Also shown as pink lines are 
      1 Gyr MIST isochrones at \(\feh = -0.5 \textrm{ (dashed) }, 
          0.0 \textrm{ (solid), and } 0.5 
  \textrm{ (dotted).}\)\label{fig:regimes}}
\end{figure}

In our sample, there are natural domains where rapid rotation can be a single
star phenomenon, a binary star phenomenon, or both. In our prior paper
\citep{Simonian19}, we found that virtually all of the photometric rapid
rotators on the lower main sequence were members of synchronized binary
systems, even though young cool stars can be rapid rotators. This is largely
because the stars targeted by the \Kepler{} mission were out of the Galactic
plane, with ages greater than 1 Gyr.

However, there is a regime where single star rapid rotation is detectable in
our sample: subgiant descendants of rapidly rotating upper main sequence stars.
Such stars were selected against by \citet{McQuillan14}, but \vsini{}
measurements have no equivalent bias.

A complicating factor is the possible presence of spurious \vsini{}
measurements photometric binaries as highlight in the Pleiades. Such binaries 
will be relatively common for an unevolved primary, but less common for an 
evolved one. To test for the presence of real astrophysical rapid rotators, 
we therefore split our \Gaia{} HR diagram into four separate categories, shown 
in \cref{fig:regimes}.

The cool dwarf regime is defined as the region near the main sequence where
stars have not evolved substantially compared to the age of the galaxy. As a
result, the observed spread in luminosity is either due to binarity or 
metallicity. We base the temperature threshold (\(\Teff < 5250\) K) for the 
cool dwarf regime both on empirical measurements of the main sequence width, 
as well as predictions from stellar isochrones as discussed in 
\citet{Simonian19}. We draw a luminosity threshold of 1.3 mag above a 1 Gyr 
solar metallicity MIST isochrone to cleanly separate the main-sequence from 
the base of the red giant branch (RGB).  Because of the high luminosity cut, 
this sample contains both photometric single and binaries, which we will 
further differentiate when needed. 

The hot dwarf regime is defined where age and binarity effects are degenerate, 
at \(\Teff > 5250\) K and located less than 1.3 mag above a 1 Gyr solar 
metallicity MIST isochrone. In this regime it is ambiguous whether a
star more luminous than the main sequence is an evolved single star with a true
radius larger than on the main sequence, or a photometric binary that should 
be deprojected. 

At higher luminosities, binarity becomes less of an issue. As the system
evolves, the primary increases in luminosity more rapidly than the secondary. 
As a result, we expect the impact of binarity to be reduced at higher
luminosities. We define a subgiant regime ranging from 1.3 -- 4.5 mag above the main 
sequence on the hot end of the regime. The maximum luminosity of the sample was
selected to separate it from the red clump. The extremely high luminosities of 
these targets imply that they must be descended from high-mass stars more 
massive than the Kraft break, and they should yield useful insights into the
evolution of angular momentum across the subgiant branch.

All other stars are considered to be giants. We don't examine them as part of
this work. Previous work have focused on rapid rotation in the APOKASC giant
sample \citep{Tayar15}.

\section{Rotation Comparison}
\label{sec:rotcomp}

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{vsini_bins}
    \caption{Placement of high \vsini{} objects on the HR diagram. The sizes of
the markers correspond to the strength of the \vsini{}. Slow rotators are
marked as grey crosses. Black lines mark locations on the HR diagram where
binaries on circular orbits with orbital periods of 1, 3, and 10 days would
undergo Roche-lobe overflow.}
    \label{fig:subgiant_rapid}
\end{figure}

Based on the rapid rotator fraction shown above, the distribution of rapid
rotators is shown in \cref{fig:subgiant_rapid} in the different regimes. Many
broad patterns can be seen in the \vsini{} distribution: cool dwarfs have few
high-vsini objects on the single-star sequence, but many on the photometric 
binary sequence, and there are a sizable population of rapidly rotating
subgiants.

In order to validate these trends, we perform the check described in 
Section~\ref{sec:astero} to establish that the \vsini{} are reliable in
different regimes in the HR diagram. We break up the analysis into
separate regimes to isolate potential systematic effects. 
For example, for stars near the main sequence, there is a population of 
photometric binaries which may cause problems for determining stellar
parameters. On the unevolved main sequence, this effect on the radius can be
corrected because the isochrones describing the single-star sequence is
insensitive to age. However, in the hot dwarf domain, there is a 
degeneracy between enhanced luminosity due to age and due to the presence of 
photometric binaries, which can complicate the interpretation of those results.

When comparing the rotational periods to \vsini{}, we do not include SB2s in
the rapid rotator fraction. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:APOGEE}, ASPCAP 
often does not recognize SB2s and will attempt to fit a single model 
atmosphere to both components. In these cases, the ASPCAP values are predicted 
to yield biased stellar parameter estimates, including \Teff, \feh, and 
abundances \citep{ElBadry18a}. While an analysis of \vsini{} was not included 
in \citet{ElBadry18a}, it's clear that \vsini{} will be overestimated for SB2s 
as ASPCAP will broaden the lines to fit them over both components. As a 
result, we separate them in the statistics in \cref{tab:rapidfrac}, and 
exclude them from our sample.

We also use isochrone radii on the unevolved lower main sequence, where age 
effects are minimal, while using the Stefan-Boltzmann law elsewhere, where 
binarity cannot be as easily corrected.  We can quantify the effect of 
photometric binaries in the hot dwarfs by comparing the pure 
photometric single population with the mixed population of photometric 
binary/evolved stars.

We demonstrate our method with the well-characterized APOKASC asteroseismic
sample. In the APOKASC sample with \citet{Garcia14} rotation periods, 73 out 
of the full 217 overlap sample have either \vsini{} detections 
(\(\vsini > 10 \kms\)) using DR14 values, or projected equatorial velocities 
greater than 10 \kms. The correspondence between the expected and observed 
velocities for the objects 
with \vsini{} detections is shown in \cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}. As expected, 
most of the stars lie below the equality line, corresponding to \(\sin i \le 
1\).  There is one object which has an unphysical solution, KIC 9908400. 
However, it may be adequately explained by contamination of the 
\Kepler{} Point Spread Function (PSF). High-resolution 
\Ks{}-band imaging from the United Kingdom Infrared Telescope clearly 
separates this target into two sources.  If the photometric variation 
originated in a cooler star within the Kepler PSF, the mismatch between the 
period and \vsini{} would be explained.

The spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction for the asteroseismic sample is 
\(19^{+3.1}_{-2.8}\)\% compared to the photometric rapid rotator fraction of 
\(32^{+3.5}_{-3.3}\)\%. After performing
the \(\sin i\) convolution described in Section~\ref{sec:veq}, we get a
predicted spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction of \(27^{+3.4}_{-3.1}\)\%, 
which is just over a \(1\sigma\) discrepancy above the measured spectroscopic 
rapid rotator fraction. We also performed this exercise using the 
\citet{McQuillan14} rotation periods, which had a smaller overlap with the 
asteroseismic sample, and yielded similar conclusions.



\subsection{Cool Dwarfs}
\label{sec:cooldwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \plotone{Cool_Dwarf_vsini_veq_comparison}
  \caption{Same as \cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}, except photometric binaries are
      marked in green. The radius is determined by deprojecting targets onto a 
      MIST isochrone as described in Section~\ref{sec:GaiaR}.\label{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}}
\end{figure}

For the cool dwarfs, we perform the comparison of the two rotational measures
using the \citet{McQuillan14} periods, the MIST-derived radii, and the APOGEE
\vsini{}. The comparison between the \vsini{} and equatorial velocity is shown
in the left panel of \cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}. SB2s behave as 
expected; the ASPCAP pipeline attempts to fit the two lines as a single peak 
and incidentally measures a high \vsini{}. The photometric binaries comprise 
a large fraction of the rapid rotators, which is consistent with the findings 
of \citet{Simonian19}. However, there is a large disagreement in both the rapid
rotator rates and in the values themselves. 

For nearly all of the spectroscopic rapid rotators, the \vsini{} 
is too large given the observed period and radius. Additionally, as shown in 
\cref{tab:rapidfrac}, the rapid rotator fraction as observed with \vsini{} is 
greater than the raw photometric rapid rotation rate by a factor of 2, a 
nearly a \(4-\sigma\) discrepancy for the corrected rotation rate. The fact
that the discrepancy is in the complete opposite direction as the \(\sin i\) 
projection indicates a very severe systematic present not seen in the 
asteroseismic sample.

We also note the presence of high \vsini{} among the population for which
\citet{McQuillan14} did not see a rotation signal. As seen in
\cref{tab:rapidfrac}, 4.5\% of the sample without significant period
detections appear to have 
high \vsini{}, which is still substantially higher than observed with photometric
rotation periods. One would expect the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction to
be much lower than the photometric rapid rotator fraction according to our 
current understanding of rotation-activity relations. Physical reasons for 
stars not having detected photometric modulation include either that the activity is low
enough that the photometric variation due to spots are below the detection
threshold of \Kepler, or that the star is aligned nearly pole-on to us. Both
outcomes are incompatible with a high \vsini{}.

Because most of the rapid rotators in this regime are photometric binaries,
it's difficult to determine in this regime whether photometric binarity is a
critical source of this systematic. There are only two spectroscopic rapid
rotators that are photometric singles, and one lies marginally over the 
\(\sin i = 1\) line and the other over the detection threshold.  As a result, 
there must be some systematic in either the \vsini{}, radius, or
rotation period to yield such a tension between the \vsini{} and rotation
period.

\label{sec:hotdwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \plotone{Hot_Dwarf_vsini_veq_comparison}
  \caption{Similar to \cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, except for the 
      hot dwarf sample. Stars which are not photometric singles are
      classified by \citet{ElBadry18b} and labeled as squares. Stars with a 
      second component in the spectrum are black, stars without a second
      component are blue, while stars that haven't been observed by

      the Stefan-Boltzmann law as described in 
  Section~\ref{sec:GaiaR}.\label{fig:hot_dwarf_velocity_comparison}}
\end{figure}

In the hot star domain we can also separate photometric singles from stars
consistent with being photometric binaries, keeping in mind that the 
photometric binary category will contain a substantial sample of genuine 
evolved single stars. We also classify the photometric binaries using the
criteria of \citet{ElBadry18b} as either being photometric binaries or
photometric singles. Stars which were not analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b} are
labeled separately. We calculate radii for the full sample by using the 
Stefan-Boltzmann Law as described in Section~\ref{sec:GaiaR}.

We plot the equatorial velocity against the measured \vsini{} for the hot 
dwarf sample in the left panel of \cref{fig:hot_dwarf_velocity_comparison}.
Unlike the cool dwarf sample, the hot dwarfs contain many more single rapid
rotators, which is to be expected because they have many stars from above the
Kraft break. We can see that both the photometric singles, and the stars
labeled by \citet{ElBadry18b} as not having contributing light from a
companion, lie in the physically reasonable regime of this diagram, where 
\(\sin i \le 1\). The photometric binary stars, on the other hand, are much 
more likely to appear in the forbidden region of this diagram with the SB2s. 
This is an indication that photometric binarity is correlated with 
disagreement between spectroscopic and photometric rapid rotation. 

The spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction for the overlap sample is 16\%, as
given in \cref{tab:rapidfrac}. The predicted \vsini{} fraction is also 16\%, which
is consistent to the \(1\sigma\) level, although it's clear from
\cref{fig:hot_dwarf_velocity_comparison} that there are individual outliers.
Splitting the sample into photometric binaries and photometric singles yields a
different story. We also compare the spectroscopic and predicted rapid rotator 
fractions for the photometric single sample, which are \(14/219 = 6.4\%\) and 
\(14.9/219 = 6.8\%\), respectively. These are consistent with each other within
\(1\sigma\). If we examine the photometric binaries separately, we get that the
spectroscopic and predicted rapid rotator rates are \(54/235 = 25\%\) and
\(38.4/235 = 16\%\), a \(1.5\sigma\) discrepancy. In fact, the number of
photometric rapid rotators is lower than the number of spectroscopic rapid
rotators, as seen in the cool dwarfs. Our finding that only the 
stars in the photometric binary regime have disagreements suggests that the
disagreement is related to the presence of a luminous companion.

We lastly look at the confluence between the two rotation measures in the

the 2844 stars in the subgiants, only 767 were analyzed by 
\citet{McQuillan14}. While the strong selection effects

rotation distribution of the subgiants as a whole, the small overlap sample

We show the comparison between the equatorial velocity and \vsini{} in

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

By comparing the agreement between the spectroscopic and photometric measures
of rotation throughout the HR diagram, we have found that there is a pattern in
which populations these measurements agree for. The two measures appear to agree well
for the asteroseismic sample, the photometric singles in the hot dwarf regime,
and for the subgiants and luminous subgiants. The \vsini{} measurements for the
cool dwarfs are broader than predicted by the rotation periods. We explore
distribution of rapid rotators in the HR diagram keeping these caveats in mind.

\begin{figure*}[htb]
    \centering
    \plottwo{rapid_rotation_heatmap}{mcq_heatmap}
    \caption{\emph{Left:} Map of the rapid rotator fraction in the HR diagram
        for the APOGEE sample using a threshold of \(\vsini = 10 \kms\). The 
        corresponding solar metallicity boundaries predicted by the models in 
        \citet{vanSaders13} for the fast and slow launch conditions are shown 
        as solid and dashed pink lines, respectively. \emph{Right:} Map of 
    rapid rotator fraction for the full \citet{McQuillan14} sample with a flat
\(v_{eq} = 10 \kms\) threshold transformed into period space. Because of the
much larger size of the \citet{McQuillan14} sample, only bins with more than 10
objects are shown. When calculating the rapid rotator fraction, targets which
did not have a significant period detection were counted as slow rotators.
Model lines are the same as on the left.}
    \label{fig:rapidfracs}
\end{figure*}

We plot the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction \(\vsini > 10 \kms\) for the 
full APOGEE sample in the left panel of \cref{fig:rapidfracs}. We find a
spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction near 100\% among the cool photometric 
binaries and a spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction near 0\% among the cool 
photometric singles, as would be expected of spectral broadening was 
associated with photometric binaries. The regime of slowly-rotating single
stars is observed to continue up until 6000 K, after which stars hotter than
the Kraft break are not expected to spin down due to surface magnetic fields. 
For the hot stars, we see a clear trend with the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction decreasing with 
decreasing temperature at fixed luminosity. The fraction of systems with 
\(\vsini > 10 \kms\) is close to 100\% for the 
hot stars, just evolving from above the Kraft break. A boundary between hot
rapid rotators and cool slow rotators is predicted from models of stellar
spindown due to a combination of expansion and increased stellar winds.

The boundary seen in the data is unlikely to be due to a smooth, 
slowly-varying background of binary contamination. The rapid rotator rates 
increases with luminosity, which is in the opposite sense as expected if it
were associated with a luminous binary companion. We also do not see this 
type of dramatic increase in the SB2s. Our measure of the rapid rotator 
fraction that we measure should also be robust to a spurious background 
population for an intrinsically rapidly rotating population. This can be seen
by considering two limiting cases: for a population with no genuine rapid 
rotators, the measured rapid rotator rate would be equal to the background 
rate of spurious rapid rotation. For a population where all stars are genuine 
rapid rotators, there would be no spurious rapid rotators. As a result, the
impact of a spurious background of rapid rotators would be to decrease the
contrast between intrinsically rapid and intrinsically slow populations.
Because we already find a rotation contrast at the location predicted by the
models, we expect the effect to be even stronger when spurious rapid rotators
are removed.

Nonetheless, as an independent check on this result, we compare the trend seen 
in \vsini{} to those seen in rotation period in the right panel of 
\cref{fig:rapidfracs}. We make the same diagram using 
the photometric temperatures from \citet{Berger18b} for the full sample 
analyzed by \citet{McQuillan14}. We count period nondetections as slow
rotators due to the period-activity relation calibrated in clusters
\citep{Hartman10}.  A similar, albeit subdued trend is seen in 
the subgiants using the rotation period data despite these stars not being 
expected to have substantial starspots. This trend can't be due to an increased
presence of hot short-period pulsators such as \(\delta\) Scutis because it
persists even with a period cut of 12 hours. The trend in decreasing rapid rotation with decreasing
temperature at fixed luminosity confirms that contamination of \vsini{} is not
very dominant in the subgiant regime.

The observation that the rapid rotator fraction is smaller for photometric
rotation periods than \vsini{} for the subgiants is expected near the location
of the Kraft break. Hotter stars transition to having radiative envelopes and 
reduced surface magnetic fields, which substantially decreases the spot
amplitudes. As a result, while most of the hot sample are likely genuine rapid
rotators, only a fraction of them can be detected from starspot modulation. 

One astrophysical background of rapid rotators we expect to persist on the
subgiant branch is a tidally-synchronized binary population on the level of 2\%
\citep{Luri18,Simonian19}. As seen from the merger limits in 
\cref{fig:subgiant_rapid}, this population is expected to persist up to the
giant branch. This population is consistent with the population of
red giant stars at the base of the giant branch, which is also consistent with
the rapid rotator rate among red giants \citep{Tayar15}. We find that for all
other evolutionary states, across both the subgiants and luminous subgiants,
there is a highly statistically-significant excess above this background
population, which we interpret as a genuine signature of single-star rotation.

On the lower main sequence, we also see a discrepancy where the spectroscopic 
rapid rotator fraction is substantially larger than the photometric rapid 
rotator fractions. Unlike with the subgiants, we argue here that photometric 
rapid rotator fraction is more representative of the true rapid rotator 
fraction because of the blending due to spectral lines noted in 
Section~\ref{sec:rotdisc}. The detection fraction for starspots is not expected
to drop dramatically in this regime like it did with the subgiants.
Additionally, all of the spectroscopic rapid rotators are location on the
photometric binary sequence, where spectral blending should have the largest
impact.

Finally, we note the presence of a nodule of rapid rotators extending down from the
giant branch at \(\Teff \sim 4400\) K and \(2 > \MK > 0\). We posit that these
are the field equivalent of sub-subgiants---tidally-synchronized evolved
subgiants which are anomalously active due to their high spin rate
\citep{Geller17,Leiner17}. 

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The spectroscopic rotation data featured a luminosity and temperature-dependent
boundary between hot, rapid rotators and cool, slow rotators which was also
seen in the photometric rotation period. This trend is likely reflective of
stellar spindown along the subgiant branch, and can be compared to models. We
also noted a substantial disagreement between rotation period and
\vsini{} in the cool dwarf regime, which we also explore more deeply. 

\subsection{Subgiant Rotation}
\label{sec:subgiants}

Knowing that the APOGEE \vsini{} yield appropriate results in the subgiant
regime, we can use the \vsini{} to validate broad trends in rotation predicted
by stellar angular momentum models \citep{vanSaders13}. It's observed that
massive stars above the Kraft break rotate extremely quickly on the main
sequence \citep{Kraft67,Abt95}. As these stars evolve onto the subgiant branch, 
one of the broad-stroke predictions of angular momentum evolution models are 
that rapid rotation can actually persist quite far along the subgiant branch.
In fact, at fixed \Teff{}, stars of higher mass are expected to have larger
rotation rates (see Fig. 2 in \citet{vanSaders13}). 

We compare the rapid rotator fraction to predictions of stellar spindown on the
subgiant branch from \citet{vanSaders13} in \cref{fig:rapidfracs}. 
\citet{vanSaders13} calculated solar-metallicity evolutionary tracks using two 
bounding sets of initial conditions: the slow and rapid envelopes on cluster rotational 
sequences (see \citet{vanSaders13} Section 2.2 for more details). For each 
launch condition, we plot an ``iso-velocity'' boundary: the location on each 
track where the star reaches a rotational velocity of 10 \kms. Regions to the
left of the slow launch model should all have \(v_{eq} > 10 \kms\); regions 
to the right of the fast launch models should all have undetectable rotation,
with a transitional region in between.

\begin{figure*}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{binned_rapid_rotators}
    \caption{Rapid rotator fraction as a function of \Teff{} for four different
    bins in luminosity. The value of the slow and fast-launch model in the
center of each bin is shown as pink vertical lines (dashed and solid). The
midpoint of the range between the highest and lowest rapid rotator fractions is
shown as a horizontal dotted line.}
    \label{fig:rrbins}
\end{figure*}

We quantify the agreement between the models and the data by comparing the
temperature where the rapid rotator fraction drops at fixed luminosity. The
rapid rotator fraction is shown in four bins of absolute magnitude and eight
bins of temperature in \cref{fig:rrbins} which span the observed subgiant 
domain. The sizes of the bins were selected to maintain a reasonable Poisson
scatter for the more luminous bin. The slow- and fast-launch boundaries of the
\citet{vanSaders13} models are shown as pink vertical lines. Stars hotter than
the slow-launch line should have a high rapid rotator fractions while stars
cooler than the fast-launch line should not have any rapid rotators with a
transitional region in-between which is dependent on the initial rotation
distribution of the sample. We mark this transition region in the data as the 
point where the rapid rotator fraction trend drops below the midpoint between
the maximum and minimum rapid rotator fraction seen at fixed luminosity.
Our sample is too small to divide into enough temperature bins to
quantitatively resolve the width of the transition region.

While the models are consistent with the data in the most luminous bin, the 
transition in 
the rapid rotator fraction for less luminous subgiants is observed to be hotter
than predicted by models, and is also substantially wider than predicted. For 
the most luminous bin, the rapid rotator fraction crosses the midpoint 
between 5600 K and 5800 K, well within the range predicted by the models. For 
the second most luminous bin, the transition region also occurs between 
5600 K and 5800 K, while the range predicted by the models is 5200--5600 K. A 
similar trend is seen in the third most luminous bin, albeit the temperature 
range predicted by the models is also smaller than the data suggests. This 
wide transition region in the least luminous bin is also seen when using 
rapid rotator fraction with the \citet{McQuillan14} rotation periods, so is 
likely real. A larger sample, particularly in the high-luminosity bin would
improve these constrains on the models. Finally we note that in the least
luminous bin, the rapid rotator fraction is flat, but nonzero, while the model
predicts that all stars should be non-rotators. We posit that this is likely
due to a combination of rotation near the Kraft break being highly uncertain
and contamination near the photometric binary sequence.

One way to determine which observed features are caused by spectral blending as
opposed to being in part of the single-star population is to use external 
markers for binarity, such as that performed by \citet{Berger18b}. 
Unfortunately, this can't be done with the \citet{Berger18b} dataset itself 
because the overlap between DR13 and DR14 for \Kepler{} subgiants is small.
However, the methodology presented in \citet{Berger18b} to flag stars with 
composite spectra can be extremely powerful for filtering out potential
contaminants in \vsini{} caused by binarity. We do not perform further modeling
of the subgiant population until the binary background can be adequately
modeled, or binaries can be reliably filtered.



\subsection{The Rotation Discrepancy}
\label{sec:rotdisc}

A discrepancy between \(v_{eq}\), \(P_{rot}\), and \(R\) would necessarily
require a systematic in one of those quantities. If the systematic was in the 
radius, a factor of several underestimation would be necessary to make the
equatorial velocity consistent with the \vsini. The radius
is determined using just two quantities: the luminosity and temperature. For 
photometric binaries, the radius would be overestimated due to
the excess luminosity of the companion. However, this can't explain the 
discrepancy because an overestimated radius implies that the true 
equatorial velocity would be smaller than the observed; exacerbating the
problem instead of improving it. The finding that the discrepancy is largest 
in the cool dwarf regime, where we correct for the excess luminosity by 
deprojecting onto an isochrone is also inconsistent with systematics in the
luminosity causing the discrepancy. Photometric binaries can also cause the APOGEE temperatures to be
underestimated by about 5\% \citep{ElBadry18a}, which translates to a 10\% 
underestimate in the radius. However, this underestimation is too small to
cause the factor of several overestimate observed.

A systematic problem in the period would require that the period was 
overestimated.  There are a number of plausible ways in which this could 
happen in cases with photometric binaries. One is that in cases where both 
binaries are spotted and have similar rotation rates, the ACF algorithm of 
\citet{McQuillan14} may detect the beat frequency of the composite signal 
rather than the rotation frequency. This can explain why the spectroscopically-derived rotation 
rate is much higher than the photometrically-derived rotation rate. In 
addition to the photometric period being overestimated, this mechanism 
would also need to explain why there are spectroscopically broadened stars 
with no photometric period at all. This may be plausible if the additional 
light of the secondary diluted the photometric period signal below the 
detection threshold of the ACF.  A more detailed analysis using the 
rotation-activity-age relationship can shed light on how blended rotation 
signals affect the detectability of rotation.  

Our favored explanation for this discrepancy is that the \vsini{} are
overestimated. In the Pleiades, we found that that 20\% of the sample were
photometric binaries, and among the photometric binaries up to 30\% had
spuriously high APOGEE \vsini, leading to a spurious \vsini{} rate of 6\% for
the full sample. We can also use a similar methodology to characterize the 
photometric binary
population in the \Kepler{} field to estimate the expected fraction of spurious
\vsini{}.. Based on the same cut of \(\Delta K < -0.3\)
mag in the \(\Delta K\) vs \(\Teff\) plane, we find that 30\% of systems are
photometric binaries. The higher fraction of photometric binaries in the
\Kepler{} field versus the Pleiades cluster is due to the well-known Malmquist
bias, where binaries can be detected to much larger volumes than single stars.
If the \vsini{} inconsistency rate among photometric binaries observed in the 
Pleiades holds in the field, we would expect between 4--9\% of the APOGEE
cool dwarfs to have spuriously large \vsini.

We interpret the anomalously high APOGEE \vsini{} as the APOGEE spectrograph
misclassifying unresolved binaries as rapid rotators. This would only occur for
binary systems for which the RV offset between the components \(\Delta v \sin 
    i = \textrm{c}/\mathcal{R} \sim 14 \kms\) is comparable to the velocity resolution
of the spectrograph. This mechanism would predict that there would be a
sharp transition in \vsini{} between this contamination of rapid rotators and
stars labeled as resolved spectroscopic binaries. As is evident from
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, there is a transition \vsini{} between
these inconsistent rapid rotators and the double-lined spectroscopic binaries.
This hypothesis can be tested with high-resolution spectroscopic follow-up of
these broad-lined targets to check if they decompose into two narrow components.
This type of contamination is most prevalent in the cool \Kepler{} dwarfs 
because true rapid rotators are intrinsically rare. In rapidly-rotating 
populations, such as the Pleiades, the rotational broadening of most stars is
already on the order of the instrumental resolution, hence the discrepancy
between the photometric and spectroscopic rotation measures would be much
smaller in these cases.

We make an order-of-magnitude estimate of the rate of this phenomenon for a 
sample of photometric binaries. We build a toy model of a population of
equal-mass \(0.5 M_\sun\) binaries in circular orbits with a period 
distribution given by the lognormal distribution in \citet{Raghavan10}. We 
model the radial velocity difference of this population by drawing 1,000,000 
randomly distributed periods, inclinations, and orbital phase using Monte 
Carlo techniques. The histogram along with the analytic estimate is shown in
\cref{fig:phot_cont}. To estimate the rate of spectral blending, we assume that
all systems where the RV offset is between 0.5--1 times the resolution of the
APOGEE spectrograph would correspond to spectral blends, which yields a rate of
10\% in our model photometric binary population. This value contrasts with the
37\% measured in the cool dwarf sample, which is surprisingly low, but still
within the same order.

We emphasize that this calculation is a very rough estimate, and did not take
into account important additional effects. First, the asserted boundaries
causing spurious \vsini{} between 0.5--1 times the resolution of the 
spectrograph are more complex in reality, and will depend sensitively on the 
signal-to-noise of the
spectrum as well as the cadence of observations in the case of multiple visits.
Additionally, eccentricity will adjust the distribution in a nontrivial way, on
one hand increasing the range of RV offsets per system, but also skewing the 
distribution of RV offsets toward smaller values. Additional modeling with the
ASPCAP pipeline is required to truly estimate the rate of spectral blends,
which is beyond the scope of this work.

While spectral blending from binaries is likely the source of the discrepancy
near the main sequence, we suggest that it is still plausible 
for the evolved stars due to the existence of SB2s at these high luminosities,
but there may be other potential contributors as well. In particular,
the sources of the rotation signal for the two methods can be different for a
binary. The \vsini{} may come from the rapidly rotating primary originating
from above the Kraft break, while the rotation period may come from a
slowly-rotating secondary originating from below the Kraft break. Without
adequate information on the binarity of these systems, we cannot determine what
is behind the discrepancy in the evolved stars. However, we note that based on
the rapid rotator fractions denoted in \cref{tab:rapidfrac}, the rate of
discrepancies is small.

We conclude that APOGEE \vsini{} measured for stars more luminous than the 
photometric binary sequence show evidence of spurious rapid rotators. For stars
closest to the photometric binary sequence, this is likely due to the 
blending of spectral lines due from a companion with unresolved radial 
velocity offsets. For more evolved stars, the persistence of SB2s shows that
this explanation may also cause spurious spectroscopic rapid rotators, but we
cannot exclude potential blending. From the presence of SB2s in the subgiant 
populations, we can also expect there to be a background of smoothly-varying 
non-astrophysical broad \vsini{} targets on the order of 7\% of the sample,
which is relatively small in the intrinsically rapidly-rotating subgiant
population.

The APOGEE sample also contains a population of \(\alpha\)-rich stars. These
stars are typically metal-poor and old \citep{Schuster12}. We evaluate whether
we see differences in spectroscopic rapid rotation patterns, which could result
from differences in angular momentum loss or binarity. The rapid rotator
fractions for the high-\(alpha\) sample are given in \cref{tab:highalphafrac}.

By comparison with \cref{tab:rapidfrac}, we find that the rapid rotator
fractions are statistically indistinguishable from the full sample in all
regimes except for the subgiants, which have a rapid-rotator fraction
that is \(2\sigma\) larger than that measured in the full 
sample. This difference can be explained if the high-alpha subgiants did not
evolve through single-star evolution, but rather through mergers of primordial
binaries. The turnoff for \(\alpha\)-enriched stars is substantially older than
that for the field in general, hence supporting the idea that these stars are
in fact, field blue stragglers, similar to blue metal-poor stars that are 
found in the halo \citep{Preston00}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared the rotation periods and spectroscopic \vsini{} for a sample of
5406 dwarfs and subgiants observed by APOGEE.\@ By comparing
the \vsini{} to previously observed dwarfs in the Pleiades with high-resolution
spectroscopy, we calibrate the APOGEE \vsini{} and determine a detection 
threshold of 10 \kms and fractional uncertainty of around 12\%. We also find 
discrepancies between the APOGEE \vsini{} and rotation periods for 
37\% of cool, photometric binaries.

We propose that the discrepancy among photometric binaries can occur either 
due to complex variation from two superimposed signals causing rotational 
periods to be overestimated, or it can be due to intermediate-period 
photometric binaries in an orbital configuration where their RV difference is 
on the order of the APOGEE velocity resolution. We favor the latter 
explanation because it is consistent with our finding of \vsini{}
inconsistencies between literature and APOGEE \vsini{} values among photometric
binaries in the Pleiades.

The discrepancy implies that APOGEE \vsini{} is a reliable marker of rotation 
for single stars but can be suspect for populations which may contain binaries.
For populations containing evolved stars, this effect is reduced, but not
eliminated. Investigations of rotation in heterogeneous stellar populations 
should consider that there will be a smoothly-varying background population of 
spurious rapid rotators for targets more luminous than the photometric binary 
sequence.

We apply this caution to comparing models of rotation in the subgiant regime 
with APOGEE \vsini{} by using a robust measure of the rapid rotator
fraction. We find that the trends of the model boundary match well with 
the empirical boundary seen in the data. A more quantitative analysis indicates
that the predicted boundary is cooler than observed by the data, and is too
narrow near the main sequence. We also find strong evidence of a spectroscopic 
rapid rotator excess near the photometric binary sequence which is not seen 
with photometric rotation periods, which is likely an artifact of spectral
blending.

We advise against making detailed comparisons between models and data in the
subgiant regime without modeling the effect of binarity, or without an
efficient way of flagging potential near-equal luminosity binaries. The
methodology in \citet{ElBadry18b} is promising for filtering binaries with
luminous companions, but needs to be run for the much larger DR14 sample in
order to be useful for the subgiant domain.
