\begin{abstract}
  We use 5466 \gvs{Double check after analysis is finalized}
  spectroscopic \vsini{} measurements of \Kepler{} dwarfs and
  subgiants from the APOGEE survey to explore stellar rotation in late-type
  dwarfs. We find a detection threshold of 10 km/s, which allows us to explore
  the spindown of intermediate-mass stars leaving the main sequence, merger
  products, young stars, and tidally-synchronized binaries. We use \Gaia{} 
  parallaxes and APOGEE temperatures to calculate radii and to search for
  photometric binaries. For the unevolved lower main sequence, we see the same
  concentration toward rapid rotation in photometric binaries as that observed
  in rotation period data, but at an enhanced rate of 26\% of photometric
  binaries. We attribute this difference to unresolved spectroscopic binaries 
  with velocity displacements on the order of the APOGEE resolution, which can
  spuriously overestimate rotational broadening. Among cool, unevolved stars 
  where rapid single star rotation is not expected, we find a detection rate of
  7.3\% \gvs{Double-check that this number of acceptable}, which we argue is a 
  measure of the impact of this phenomenon. For the subgiants, we use asteroseismic data to
  demonstrate that period and \vsini{} methods agree, indicating the \vsini{}
  measurements are less impacted by the presence of binaries for these targets. 
  We see clear evidence for a transition between rapid and slow rotation on the
  subgiant branch in the domain predicted by modern angular momentum evolution
  models, with detections on the hot side much larger than the background from
  binary stars. We also find substantial agreement between the spectroscopic
  and photometric properties of KIC targets added by \citet{Huber14} based on
  2MASS photometry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rotation is a fundamental property of stars. Stars are born with a wide range 
of rotation rates as they leave the pre-main sequence \citep{Attridge92,
Herbst00, Henderson12}. Once on the main sequence, \citet{Kraft67} noticed a
dichotomy in rotation rates, with higher-mass stars maintaining a wide range of
rotation rates, while cooler stars rotate substantially more slowly. The
transition between the two is thought to be because of the onset of convective
envelopes in lower-mass stars, which lose angular momentum through magnetized
winds \citep{Parker58,Weber67,Kawaler88}. 

Rotation can be measured in two different ways. One method measures
the projected broadening of spectral lines due to rotation \citep{Kahler89},
which requires spectroscopy.  An additional method
which has been expanded greatly by the \Kepler{} satellite is the measurement
of rotation periods through modulations in the light curves of starspots
\citep{Basri11,Affer12,Nielsen13,Reinhold13,McQuillan14,Garcia14}.
These measurements take advantage of long stretches of precise and continuous
coverage. Unlike the spectral lines, rotational periods are sensitive to
much slower rotation rates because rotational effects become comparable to
other line broadening mechanisms at the few \kms level. 
However, they can only be measured for cool stars with spots, and detections at
low levels become challenging because of systematic uncertainties in the
differential photometry (see \citet{vanSaders19} for a discussion)

Rotation is valuable for understanding stellar physics not just because it can
tell us about stellar dynamos and winds, but also because rotation may yield 
inferences of stellar ages, a
technique known as gyrochronology \citep{Barnes07,Mamajek08}. By
studying the evolution of rotation in open clusters, gyrochronology relations
have been derived to infer ages from rotation rates and masses. However, open
cluster rotation measurements are primarily in young systems, where amplitudes
are high and periods are short, with few old calibrators available. Attempts to 
calibrate the relations using \Kepler{} stars with 
asteroseismic ages revealed challenges in extending existing gyrochronology
relationships \citep{Angus15,vanSaders16}.

With the release of substantial rotation data with \Kepler{}, APOGEE, and
\TESS{}, it is possible to
study departures from standard rotational stellar evolution. The advent of
\Gaia{} parallaxes allows discrimination between dwarfs and subgiants in the
\Kepler{} field \citep{Berger18b}. This discrimination not only helps provide 
more reliable inferences from gyrochronology on populations, but can also be 
used to evaluate modern models of subgiant rotation \citep{vanSaders13}.
\citet{Simonian19} also provided evidence that the photometric rapid
rotators in the \Kepler{} field were not young stars, but were consistent with 
being a population of tidally-synchronized binaries. 

Most studies of stellar rotation for cool stars have focused on young, solar
metallicity clusters \citet{Gallet15}. As a result, there is little known about
how the behavior of stellar rotation changes with metallicity. One of the
challenges of measuring the metallicity dependence of stellar rotation is that
the rotation periods and metallicity must be measured separately; as a
practicality, this leads to little overlap between samples. On the other hand,
metallicities often accompany measurements of spectroscopic \vsini{}.
Multiplexed high-resolution spectroscopy, such as that done by the APOGEE will
be useful for measuring the metallicity dependence of rotation. 

While there have been several attempts to evaluate the rotation
periods of \Kepler{} stars, they have all been through
cross-comparing different periodogram analyses, but not with independent 
measures of rotation, such as \vsini. The advent of large spectroscopic
surveys, such as the Apache Point Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE), has
enabled the measurement of \vsini{} for hundreds of thousands of stars.
However, the methodology to measure \vsini{} in APOGEE differs from that
traditionally used in the rotation literature. APOGEE performs a \(\chi^2\) 
minimization on the observed spectra rather than cross-correlating with
standards \citep{Gray08}. We demonstrate that this methodology yields consistent
\vsini{} to the traditional method.

In this paper, we compare the rotation distribution of \Kepler{} targets to
that seen from APOGEE \vsini{}s. Section~\ref{sec:sample} describes the data 
necessary to perform the analysis in this work. We validate the uncertainties 
and detection thresholds of \vsini{} and 2MASS-based temperatures in 
Section~\ref{sec:validation}. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the
distribution of rapid rotators. We then discuss the distribution of rapid
rotators and compare to models in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Finally, we
summarize and conclude in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Catalog Data}
\label{sec:sample}

The base sample for our study of rotation in the \Kepler{} field is the 
sample of \Kepler{} stars observed by APOGEE. The selection
function for this sample is described in \citet{Zasowski17}. The majority of
targets were chosen for asteroseismic studies of evolved red giants, but 7,918 
unevolved stars were targeted by the survey, and as of DR14 3,575 have been
observed. The APOGEE spectra provide stellar parameters, as well as rotational 
broadenings. 

Although the APOGEE spectra have yielded reliable \vsini{} for evolved stars
\citep{Tayar15}, the same is not has not been shown for dwarfs. Fortunately, 
there is a significant overlap of 1,054 dwarfs between the targeted APOGEE 
sample and the rotation period sample of \citet{McQuillan14} to calibrate the
\vsini{}. To compare these two measures statistically, we estimate stellar 
radii from 2MASS photometry, \Gaia{} astrometry, and APOGEE spectroscopy. The details of 
the input catalogs are described in more detail 
below. 

\subsection{APOGEE}
\label{sec:APOGEE}

The underlying sample is drawn from the Data Release 14 \citep{Abolfathi18}
of the APOGEE survey \citep{Majewski17}, hereafter DR14. The workhorse behind 
the survey is the APOGEE spectrograph, a high-resolution (\(R \sim 22,000\)) 
multi-fiber near-infrared spectrograph \citep{Wilson10} mounted on the SDSS 
2.5-meter telescope at Apache Point Observatory \citep{Gunn06}.


APOGEE stellar parameters are derived from the ASPCAP pipeline
\citep{GarciaPerez16}. ASPCAP measures stellar parameters by performing a
chi-squared minimization \citep{AllendePrieto06} over a grid of stellar
atmospheres in three runs that includes \vsini{} for dwarfs.

Comparisons of APOGEE \Teff{} to literature studies with high-resolution
spectroscopy found that the temperature is consistent to within the
uncertainties of around 80 K with a possible 20 K zero-point shift, which is
very small for our purposes. However, the reported uncertainty in \feh{} was 
found to be underestimated, and the true random error is closer to 0.1 dex 
\citep{Serenelli17}. The APOGEE \vsini{} measurements just became included as 
catalog values in DR14.  \citet{Tayar15} measured \vsini{} directly from the 
ASPCAP spectra and validated them for
evolved stars. We therefore validate the catalog \vsini{} for 
main-sequence stars in Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

We inspected the fits to a set of spectra representative of targets flagged 
by the ASPCAP pipeline as having potential quality problems. A small fraction
(2.6\%) of targets have the \STARBAD{} quality flag enabled. This quality flag
is triggered when any of the model parameters hit a grid edge, the chi-square
of the fit is poor, the spectrum has poor signal to noise, or if the
color temperature of the targets are substantially different from the
spectroscopic temperature. Visual inspection of a representative sample of 
spectra indicates that the flag is largely the result of poor subtraction or 
normalization of the spectrum in the pipeline, or due to poor model fits on the 
cool (\(\Teff < 4250 K\)) end. However, there is a small fraction of objects
where the \STARBAD{} flag was only triggered because the \vsini{} hit a grid
edge. We manually calibrate stellar parameters for these stars 
\citep{Holtzman18}, and treat those few targets as \vsini{} lower limits. The
remaining \STARBAD{} objects are excluded from the sample. We did not remove
targets with the \STARWARN{} flag because visual inspection of a representative
sample of these spectra indicates that the fits reasonably resemble the
underlying spectra.

Because we are interested in \vsini{} for this analysis, one potentially
significant class of contaminants are double-lined spectroscopic binaries
(SB2s). The ASPCAP pipeline derived stellar parameters by fitting a single-star
stellar atmosphere to spectra. For spectra with two sets of lines, it will 
attempt to fit an extremely broad single line profile on top of both sets of 
lines, resulting in a spurious rapid rotator. We visually inspected the 
spectra of all targets in our sample with 
\vsini{} greater than \(10 \kms\) (the detection threshold determined in
Section~\ref{sec:vsinival}) to flag them in our sample. If a given star showed 
signatures of double-lined spectra
either in the combined spectrum, or in any of the individual visit spectra, it
was flagged as a visual SB2. An illustrative example of each case is shown in 
\cref{fig:sb2diag}.  

We also note that the dwarf-giant separation occurs along the base of the red 
giant branch, as is illustrated in the right panel of \cref{fig:regimes}, which
would make the APOGEE sample biased against rapid rotators in that regime,
since rotation is not measured. Red giant rotation in this sample was studied
by \citet{Tayar15}, and we refer the interested reader to that paper for those
targets.

\subsubsection{Binarity}

\citet{ElBadry18b} ran a machine-learning algorithm to flag
stars which had spectra that were better fit with a binary model than with a 
single star model.  For stars with poor single-star fits, \citet{ElBadry18b} 
attempted to find a better-fitting model. Additional models that they 
considered were: SB1,
SB2, SB2 with hidden triple component, and SB3. For the best-fit model,
\citet{ElBadry18b} fitted a wider set of parameters for each component.
\citet{ElBadry18b} only ran their analysis on the DR13 release, which has 
substantially fewer dwarfs and subgiants than the DR15 release. As a result, 
only 17\% of our sample was analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b}.
While this method yields extremely precise estimates, determining the true 
errors in these parameters is extremely difficult. As a result, we forgo 
using the revised quantities, and instead use the binarity flags in 
\citet{ElBadry18b} to distinguish single stars from photometric binaries. We 
label stars in the classes ``Single'' and ``SB1'' as
being ``\citet{ElBadry18b} Singles'', because there is very little flux
contributed from the companion. The classes ``SB2'', ``hidden triple'' and
``SB3'' are considered ``\citet{ElBadry18b} SB2s'' because there is measurable
flux from the companion in the spectrum.

\subsection{Rotation Periods}
\label{sec:periods}

The main sample of rotation periods in this work comes from
\citet{McQuillan14}. The primary selection criteria for targets to be analyzed
in \citet{McQuillan14} was to distinguish between dwarfs and giants by using
the \Teff-\logg{} cuts advocated by \citet{Ciardi11}. In order to avoid 
confusion with other periodic signals, \citet{McQuillan14} removed known 
eclipsing binaries \citep{Prsa11,Slawson11} and KOIs \citep{Akeson13}. They 
also imposed a \Teff{} limit of less than 6500 K to reduce confusion with 
pulsators. Their final sample to be analyzed contained 133,030 stars.

\citet{McQuillan14} calculated rotation periods using an autocorrelation
function (ACF) based method. The result 
was a list of 34,030 stars with period detections, as well as a list of 
99,000 stars with no significant period detection.

In addition to the \citet{McQuillan14} periods, we also make use of surface 
rotation periods derived for a sample of 310 \Kepler{} stars showing
asteroseismic oscillations \citep{Garcia14}, 217 of which have been observed
with APOGEE. Unlike the methodology of
\citet{McQuillan14}, which was based on the autocorrelation of light curves,
the analysis in \citet{Garcia14} is based on wavelet analysis. We use 
\citet{Garcia14} data as a basis dataset for the APOKASC asteroseismic sample 
for which they were calculated. The asteroseismic sample with rotation periods
in \citet{Garcia14} is shown in the left-hand panel of \cref{fig:regimes}.

\subsection{Astrometry}
\label{sec:astrometry}

The availability of high-quality parallaxes from the \Gaia{} mission's Data
Release 2 \citep{Gaia18} enables both the determination of
radii for the APOGEE sample, as well as evolutionary state classifications for
stars. The \Gaia{} DR2 performed a fully consistent single-star
5-parameter (\(\alpha\), \(\delta\), \(\mu_\alpha\), \(\mu_\delta\), \(\pi\))
solution to 1.3 billion sources over 22 months of observations
\citep{Lindegren18}. 

We use the cross-matched database of \citet{Berger18b} to match \Kepler{} targets
against \Gaia{} DR2 detections. Of the 16,915 objects observed by DR14, 778 did not make it in the
catalog by \citet{Berger18b}. Of the 778, only 273 are consistent with not
being giants according to the classification in \citet{Holtzman18}. 46 of 
these objects have no counterpart in \Gaia{} DR2. The rest were excluded by 
the \citet{Berger18b} quality cuts.  Because the targets in \citet{Berger18b} 
have high-quality parallaxes, we use the traditional formula for deriving 
distance modulus from parallax instead of the Bayesian method advocated by 
\citet{Luri18} to reduce computational complexity. We include a zero-point 
offset of 50.2 \(\mu\)as determined from comparisons to asteroseismic radii 
\citep{Zinn18}.

\subsection{Photometry}
\label{sec:photometry}

In order to characterize our stellar sample, we choose to use 2MASS
\Ks-band photometry \citep{Skrutskie06} to infer bolometric luminosity, which minimizes the impact 
of extinction. A very small fraction of targets (0.3\%) of the 
APOGEE sample have 2MASS photometry flagged due to blending. In order to 
ensure that all the photometry in our sample is uniformly analyzed, we 
excluded these blended objects. 

\subsection{Extinction}
\label{sec:extinction}

We use estimates of extinction from the \citet{Berger18b} catalog. They
interpolated reddening vectors of \(A_V\) based on the 3-D reddening map of
\citet{Green18}. The median extinction (with \(1\sigma\) quartile ranges) for
the sample of non-giants in our sample is \(0.12^{+0.12}_{-0.04}\). Applying 
the \citet{Cardelli89} relation that \(A_K/A_V = 0.114\), the effect of 
extinction drops to only a factor of four larger than the average photometric 
uncertainties of 0.017 mag in our sample. 

\subsection{Photometric Temperature}

For stars which do not have APOGEE spectroscopic temperatures, we use the
\citet{Berger18b} photometric temperatures, which are available for nearly the
entire \Kepler{} sample. The \citet{Berger18b} temperatures are updated versions
of the temperatures determined by the Kepler Stellar Parameter Catalog 
\citep{Mathur17}, using the methodology of \citet{Huber14} and temperature
scale of \citet{Pinsonneault12}


\subsection{Comparison Datasets}

In order to validate the quantities used for our analysis, we compare the
quantities used and derived in this study with previous estimates to
understand their accuracy and any systematic uncertainties. The main 
quantities we compare are the APOGEE \vsini{}, the
\citet{McQuillan14} rotation periods, and stellar radii.

Since the introduction of \vsini{} in the Data Release 14 in APOGEE, it has 
only gone through a limited amount of validation and calibration 
\citep{Tayar15}. In order to better understand the characteristics of \vsini{} 
measurements in APOGEE, we compare the APOGEE \vsini{} to other overlapping 
large spectroscopic surveys. By comparing the APOGEE \vsini{} to \vsini{} 
measured by other instruments with known characteristics, we can estimate 
both the uncertainty in the APOGEE \vsini{} as well as the detection threshold 
for APOGEE.

Because the sample analyzed in this work consists of stars across the HR
diagram, we try to also perform a comparison for stars on the main 
sequence. \citet{Tayar15} performed a comparison between the APOGEE and 
spectroscopic sample of asteroseismic subgiants in \citet{Bruntt12}. However, 
there are not any high-resolution spectroscopic surveys of unevolved stars in 
the \Kepler{} field which have a high enough overlap with 
APOGEE to have a substantial fraction of \vsini{} detections. The California 
Kepler Survey \citep{Petigura17}, for example, does not have any stars with
detectable rotation in 
common with APOGEE\@. However, a large overlap 
sample exists in
the Pleiades, which has been the subject of extensive rotational velocity
surveys \citep{Stauffer84,Stauffer87,Soderblom93,Queloz98,Terndrup00,Jackson18}. 
We select stars which were observed both in APOGEE as well as in at least one 
of these previous studies, cross-matching using the master table in
\citet{Rebull16a}, which yields a total overlap of 361 observations of 152 
targets. A detailed breakdown of the overlap with each survey is given in 
\cref{tab:pleiadescount} We use the \vsini{} uncertainty reported in each study if available. 
In order to distinguish
photometric binaries from photometric single stars, we use the displacement
from the main sequence (\(\Delta V\) calculated in \citet{Rebull16a}, and take 
    advantage of the fact that at a constant \(V-\Ks\), \(\Delta V = \Delta
\Ks\)).


In addition to the \vsini{}, we are also able to evaluate the performance of
the \citet{Huber14} determination of temperatures with poor KIC photometry. The
original KIC had 2,315 targets without \Teff{} determinations in the KIC,
for which \citet{Huber14} inferred \Teff{} and evolutionary state from just 
2MASS colors. APOGEE observed 2,045 of them because they were bright
and encountered saturation problems \citep{Huber14}. We will briefly touch on 
a comparison between the two datasets in Section~\ref{sec:huber}.

SB2s whose radial velocity effect is comparable to, but not resolved by the
APOGEE spectrograph. When using APOGEE \vsini{} care must be
taken to either filter or model stars with luminous companions from the 
sample.

\subsection{\vsini{} Validation}
\label{sec:vsinival}

APOGEE \vsini{} measurements have been used and validated for giants and
subgiants \citep{Tayar15}, but not for dwarfs. \citet{Tayar15} derived \vsini{} by
cross-correlating APOGEE spectra, and found good agreement with
\vsini{} values from high-resolution spectroscopy of subgiants \citep{Bruntt12}, 
with a robust detection threshold of \(7 \kms\), and uncertainty of 10\%.

    \centering

  calculated in \citet{Stauffer16} are denoted by green points. A
  representative error bar is shown in the upper right.}

To validate the APOGEE \vsini{} measurements for dwarfs, we use the 
well-studied Pleiades cluster. The studies that we draw from
are \citet{Stauffer84,Stauffer87,Soderblom93,Queloz98,Terndrup00,Jackson18}. 
The HR diagrams for the six samples are shown in \cref{fig:pleiades_hr}.

\begin{figure*}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{vsinicomp}
  \caption{\emph{Left:} Comparison of APOGEE \vsini{} to the literature 
      \vsini{} for photometric singles in each study denoted in
      \cref{fig:pleiades_hr}. Grey points are below the APOGEE detection 
      limit of 10~\kms.  Left-pointing and right-pointing arrows are reported

      as middle except for the photometric binary sample.}
  \label{fig:vsinicomp}
\end{figure*}

Before comparing \vsini{}, we separate the sample into photometric binaries and
photometric singles. We consider objects in the Pleiades to be photometric 
binaries if they lie greater than 0.3 mag above the
main-sequence locus in \(\Delta V\) vs \(V-K\) as measured by 
\citet{Stauffer16}. We perform the cut using the \(V-K\) color rather than the
\Teff{} shown in \cref{fig:pleiades_hr} because the impact of a companion on
\(V-K\) color is more predictable than on the \Teff{} inferred from the APOGEE
spectrum \citep{ElBadry18a,Simonian19}. The photometric binaries consist of 
21\% of the Pleiades \vsini{} sample. 

We compare the APOGEE \vsini{} to the literature \vsini{} in the left panel
of \cref{fig:vsinicomp}. We find that in all datasets, there is an apparent
increase in the variance between the APOGEE and literature \vsini{} at around
\(10 \kms\). This \(10 \kms\) threshold is larger than the \(7
\kms\) threshold adopted by \citet{Tayar15}. This is largely because giants are
sharp-lined objects, and it is easier to measure broadening in them than in
dwarf stars with larger line broadening. 


We determined the APOGEE uncertainty by modeling the literature and APOGEE
observations as independent observations with lognormally-distributed errors
for the \citet{Stauffer87}, \citet{Queloz98}, and \citet{Jackson18} samples. We
choose those three samples because there is a large overlap with APOGEE, 
unlike with the \citet{Stauffer84} and \citet{Terndrup00} samples, and 
because they report uncertainties, unlike \citet{Soderblom93}.
We used an MCMC routine \citep{ForemanMackey13} with a likelihood function:
\begin{equation}
    L(\{v_{1,i}\}, \{v_{2,i}\} | \{\sigma_{1,i}\}, \sigma_2, \{v_{i}\}) =
    \prod_{i} \mathcal{N}(\log v_{1,i} ; \log v_i, \sigma_{1,i}) \mathcal{N}(
    \log v_{2,i}; \log v_i, \sigma_2)
\end{equation}
where \(\{v_{1,i}\}, \{v_{2,i}\}\) and \(\sigma_{1,i}\) are the known
    literature \vsini{}, APOGEE \vsini{}, and literature \vsini{} error, 
    \(\{v_i\}\) are the unobserved true vsini, and \(\sigma_2\) is the
    uncertainty we are trying to derive. We adopt flat priors for all
    \(\{v_i\}\) and \(\sigma_2\) between \(\log_{10}(1)\) and 
    \(\log_{10}(100)\). 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{Pleiades_Uncertainty}
    \caption{Distribution of fractional uncertainty derived using MCMC for the 
        APOGEE catalog \vsini{} based on comparisons with \citet{Stauffer87} 
        (blue), \citet{Queloz98} (red), and \citet{Jackson18} (green). A 
        weighted combination of the three distributions is shown in black, 
        which has a median value of \(11.7^{+2.4}_{--2.5}\) with \(1\sigma\) 
    confidence intervals. }
    \label{fig:sigvsini}
\end{figure}

Fitting the fractional uncertainty to each of the datasets separately yields
the distribution shown in \cref{fig:sigvsini}. For the three datasets we used
for this analysis, the derived fractional uncertainties were 
\(12.2^{+2.0}_{-1.7}\), \(9.5^{+2.1}_{-1.7}\), and \(13.0^{+2.2}_{-1.9}\) 
As a result, we consider a reasonable uncertainty to be 12\%, about the order 
suggested by \citet{Tayar15}. We do not combine the analysis from the three
datasets because it is beyond the scope of this analysis to model potential
systematics between the three datasets.

The photometric binaries, shown in the right panel of \cref{fig:vsinicomp},
have a much larger scatter than the photometric single stars. We estimate the 
rate of \vsini{} inconsistencies among the binaries by 
calculating a \(\chi^2\) figure of merit between the
literature and APOGEE \vsini{} values for all targets with APOGEE
\(\vsini > 10 \kms\). If the \vsini{} values are more than
\(3\sigma\) discrepant, then we flag them as inconsistent. If the same star was
measured in multiple datasets, we classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as consistent,
if the \vsini{} measurement was within \(3\sigma\) for all datasets, we
classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as inconsistent if it is inconsistent at the
\(3\sigma\) level for all datasets, and classify the APOGEE \vsini{} as
ambiguous if it is consistent with some datasets and inconsistent with others.
We don't include measurements from \citep{Soderblom93} because they did not
include uncertainties in their \vsini{} measurements. In our sample, we have 
24 photometric binaries with \vsini{} detections in APOGEE and in at least 
one literature source. Of them, 17 are consistent, 3 are inconsistent, and 4
are ambiguous. Discounting the ambiguous cases, we 
estimate that \(15^{+12}_{-8.1}\%\) of photometric binaries have \vsini{} 
measured in APOGEE that is inconsistent with that found in the literature. By
contrast, there are no inconsistent cases in the photometric single stars.


\subsection{Comparison with Rotation Periods}
\label{sec:rotcomp}

\input{tables/rapidfrac_dwarfs.tex}
\input{tables/rapidfrac_evolved.tex}

In this section, we compare the photometric rotation periods and spectroscopic
\vsini{} on a point by point basis. Unlike when comparing to literature
\vsini{} measurements, the \(\sin i\) ambiguity means that individual
\vsini{} values can only be excluded for being too fast. To capture the
statistical properties needed to ensure both measurements are consistent on the
whole, we compare the spectroscopic and photometric rapid 
rotator fractions for all stars in common between APOGEE and
\citet{McQuillan14} to determine to determine how well these two rotation
measures agree. 

When calculating the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction we do not include
visual SB2s, as ASPCAP will attempt to fit a single model 
atmosphere to both components, making the \vsini{} unreliable. As a 
result, we separate them in the statistics in
\cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf,tab:rapidfracevolved}, and exclude them from our 
sample.

We compare rotation periods to \vsini{} by using the relation:
\begin{equation}
    \log v_{eq} = \log 2 \pi R - \log P
    \label{eq:veq}
\end{equation}
where \(R\) is the stellar radius, and \(P\) is the rotation period, then
convolving each of the equatorial velocities with a \(\sin i\) distribution to
calculate a predicted spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction given the rotation
period distribution. 

We first calculate isochrone-independent 
radii by making use of the K-band luminosity and temperature through the 
Stefan-Boltzmann law:
\begin{equation}
    \log R = \log L_\sun - 0.4 \left(\Ks +5 \log \left(\frac{\omega}{100
    \textrm{ mas }} \right) + BC(\Teff, \feh) - M_{bol,\sun}\right) - 4 \log
        \Teff - \log 4\pi\sigma
\end{equation}
where \Ks{} is the 2MASS \Ks-band flux, \(\omega\) is the \Gaia{} parallax,
\(BC\) is the bolometric correction in the \Ks-band, \Teff{} is the effective temperature,
\feh{} is the metallicity, \(M_{bol},\sun=4.74\) is the absolute bolometric 
magnitude of the Sun \citep{Mamajek15}, and \(\sigma\) is the Stefan-Boltzmann 
constant. All of these quantities except for the bolometric correction are available in the 
combined APOGEE/\Gaia/2MASS catalogs. 

We calculate the bolometric correction using the ATLAS12/SYNTHE 
\citet{Kurucz70, Kurucz93} stellar atmosphere models included with the MIST
isochrones. We interpolate over \Teff{} to yield bolometric corrections. To 
gauge the systematic uncertainty 
in the MIST bolometric correction, we compare the MIST bolometric correction 
to the bolometric corrections reported in \citet{Casagrande10} at three
different metallicities, and find that the systematic uncertainty in the
bolometric correction is about 0.01 mag, which is negligible for our purposes.
We also compare our \Ks-band radius to the SED-fitted radius in \citet{Berger18b}
and find that our radii and those from \citet{Berger18b} are consistent to 
within the formal errors with a scatter of 
3\%, which is substantially smaller than the random errors imposed by \Teff{} 
and the potential effect of binarity (see \citet{Simonian19} for a discussion).

For the \Kepler{} cool dwarf and photometric binary sample, we derive radii 
by assuming that the single star relation can be characterized by the MIST 
isochrones. 
A few representative 1 Gyr isochrones are shown in the left panel of 
\cref{fig:regimes}. We interpolate on a 1 Gyr solar metallicity isochrone 
given an object's \Teff{}, as a single metallicity isochrone yields more
accurate results than adjusting for the metallicity \citep{Simonian19}. 

The rotation periods in \Kepler{} have extensive 
cross-comparisons and the agreement in general is quite good for rapid rotators
like those investigated in this work, with autocorrelation function and 
wavelet methods performing best. \citet{Aigrain15}
injected rotation signals into quiescent \Kepler{} light curves and based upon
a blinded search found that the \citet{McQuillan14} algorithm identified 
rotation periods in 100\% of the samples which had them, and had an 80\% 
reliability in identifying the true rotation period within the given 
uncertainties. The analysis with the \citet{Garcia14} method was not blinded,
but yielded a detection fraction of 78\% and an 88\% reliability; however,
rotation periods based on the \citet{Garcia14} algorithm are not yet available
for the full \Kepler{} field.

Our chosen detection limit of 10 \kms{} translates to a 
radius-dependent period threshold illustrated in \cref{fig:period_vel}. 
The period cutoff is 3.7 days for a \(R = 0.72 R_\sun\) star, typical of the 
cool dwarf and photometric binary samples; 6.2 days for a \(R = 1.2 R_\sun\) 
hot dwarf; 11.3 days for 
a \(R = 2.2 R_\sun\) subgiant;  and 23.5 days for a
\(R = 4.6 R_\sun\) lower RGB star. The photometric rapid rotator fraction
corresponding to a detection threshold of 10 \kms for each regime is given as 
the second line of \cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf,tab:rapidfracevolved}. 

After calculating the equatorial velocity, given the period
and radius, we convolve it with a \(\sin i\) distribution, and calculate how
much of the convolved distribution remains above the
\vsini{} detection limit of 10 \kms. Previous attempts to compare rotation periods to
\vsini{} in clusters found that starspot modulations are not detected for stars
which are inclined by \(\sin i < 0.5\), so we truncate our \(\sin i\)
convolution kernel at \(\sin i = 0.5\) \citep{Krishnamurthi97,Jackson10}. The predicted
\vsini{} distribution is given as the third line in
\cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf,tab:rapidfracevolved}.

We demonstrate our method with the well-characterized asteroseismic dwarf
sample. Of the 217 asteroseismic dwarfs with \citet{Garcia14} rotation 
periods, 73 have either \vsini{} detections 
(\(\vsini > 10 \kms\)) using DR14 values, or projected equatorial velocities 
greater than 10 \kms. The correspondence between the expected and observed 
velocities for the objects 
with \vsini{} detections is shown in \cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}. As expected, 
most of the stars lie below the equality line, corresponding to \(\sin i \le 
1\).  There is one object which has an unphysical solution, KIC 9908400. 
However, it may be adequately explained by contamination of the 
\Kepler{} Point Spread Function (PSF). High-resolution 
\Ks{}-band imaging from the United Kingdom Infrared Telescope clearly 
separates this target into two sources.  If the photometric variation 
originated in a cooler star within the Kepler PSF, the mismatch between the 
period and \vsini{} would be explained.

The spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction for the asteroseismic sample is 
\(16.4^{+2.9}_{-2.6}\)\% compared to the photometric rapid rotator fraction of 
\(28.9^{+3.3}_{-3.1}\)\%. After performing
the \(\sin i\) convolution described in Section~\ref{sec:rotcomp}, we get a
predicted spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction of \(23.6^{+3.2}_{-2.9}\)\%, 
which is just over a \(1\sigma\) discrepancy above the measured spectroscopic 
rapid rotator fraction. We also performed this exercise using the 
\citet{McQuillan14} rotation periods, which had a smaller overlap with the 
asteroseismic sample, and yielded similar conclusions.

temperatures for stars which previously only had \Teff{} and evolutionary

those parameters to determine the reliability of stellar classifications based 
only on 2MASS photometry.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{vsini_bins}
    \caption{Placement of high \vsini{} objects on the HR diagram. The sizes of
the markers correspond to the strength of the \vsini{}. Stars flagged as visual 
SB2s are light blue stars. Slow rotators are
marked as grey crosses. }
    \label{fig:vsini_bins}
\end{figure}

Unlike in the Pleiades, which has a single-age stellar population, the
\Kepler{} field is a heterogeneous mixture of stars of different ages and
metallicities. As a result, interpreting the rotation distribution is
substantially more complex. For example, while photometric binaries, subgiants,
and main sequence stars in a cluster separate easily for all masses except near
the turnoff mass, they overlap substantially in the \Kepler{} field due to the
variegated ages. 

In our prior paper
\citep{Simonian19}, we found that virtually all of the photometric rapid
rotators on the lower main sequence were members of synchronized binary
systems, even though young cool stars can be rapid rotators. This is largely
because the stars targeted by the \Kepler{} mission were out of the Galactic
plane, with ages greater than 1 Gyr.

However, there is a regime where single star rapid rotation is detectable in
our sample: subgiant descendants of rapidly rotating upper main sequence stars.
Such stars were selected against by \citet{McQuillan14}, but \vsini{}
measurements have no equivalent bias.

By comparing the spectroscopic and photometric rapid rotation rates in the
overlap sample with \citet{McQuillan14}, for all regimes except the photometric
binaries, which have an excess of spectroscopic rapid rotators, and the RGB
base, which has a deficit of rapid rotators. As discussed in
\cref{sec:evolvedstars}, both the spectroscopic and photometric rotation
methods are poorly sampled in this regime, so there isn't much information in
this analysis. 

Generalizing to the full APOGEE sample (regardless of \citet{McQuillan14}), we
determine in \cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf} that \(25.9^{+3.6}_{-3.4}\%\) of the 
photometric binaries are spectroscopic rapid rotators, a large fraction of 
which are spurious). We can get an upper limit on the fraction of spurious
\vsini{} measurements expected in a mixed population, such as the hot dwarfs or
subgiants, by combining the counts from the photometric binaries and cool
dwarfs. Doing so yields an upper limit of \(7.3^{+1.1}_{-1.0}\%\). In the hot
dwarf and subgiant regimes, the spectroscopic rapid rotator fractions are
\(9.9^{+0.8}_{-0.7}\%\) and \(21.7^{+1.4}{-1.4}\%\). Since both are larger than
the upper limit of 7.3\%, we claim the detection of a population of genuine
rapid rotators in the hot stars and subgiants, which we explore in more detail.

\subsection{Subgiant Rotation}
\label{sec:subgiants}

We plot the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction for the 
full APOGEE sample in the left panel of \cref{fig:rapidfracs}. We find a
spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction near 100\% among the cool photometric 
binaries and a spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction near 0\% among the cool 
photometric singles, as would be expected of spectral broadening was 
associated with photometric binaries. The regime of slowly-rotating single
stars is observed to continue up until 6000 K, above which stars hotter than
the Kraft break are not expected to spin down.
For the hot stars, we see a clear trend of the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction decreasing with 
decreasing diagonally from hot, luminous stars to cool dwarfs. The fraction of 
systems with \(\vsini > 10 \kms\) is close to 100\% for the 
hot stars, just evolving from above the Kraft break. A transition region 
between hot rapid rotators and cool slow rotators is predicted from models of 
stellar spindown due to a combination of expansion and increased stellar 
winds.

The boundary seen in the data is unlikely to be due to a smooth, 
slowly-varying background of binary contamination. The rapid rotator rates 
increases with luminosity, which is in the opposite sense as expected if it
were associated with a luminous binary companion. We also do not see this 
type of dramatic increase in the visual SB2s (see \cref{fig:vsini_bins}).
Finally, our measure of the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction should be 
robust to a spurious background population for an intrinsically rapidly 
rotating population. This can be seen
by considering two limiting cases: for a population with no genuine rapid 
rotators, the measured rapid rotator rate would be equal to the background 
rate of spurious rapid rotation. For a population where all stars are genuine 
rapid rotators, no rapid rotators would be spurious. As a result, the
impact of a spurious background of rapid rotators would be to decrease the
contrast between intrinsically rapid and intrinsically slow populations, not
increase it.
Because we already find a rotation contrast at the location predicted by the
models, we expect the effect to be even stronger when spurious rapid rotators
are removed.

Nonetheless, as an independent check on this result, we compare the trend seen 
in \vsini{} to those seen in rotation period in the right panel of 
\cref{fig:rapidfracs}. We make the same diagram using 
the photometric temperatures from \citet{Berger18b} for the full sample 
analyzed by \citet{McQuillan14}. We count period nondetections as slow
rotators due to the period-activity relation calibrated in clusters
\citep{Hartman10}.  A similar, albeit subdued trend is seen in 
the subgiants using the rotation period data despite these stars not being 
expected to have substantial starspots. This trend can't be due to an increased
presence of hot short-period pulsators such as \(\delta\) Scutis because it
persists even with a period cut of 12 hours. The trend in decreasing rapid rotation with decreasing
temperature at fixed luminosity confirms that contamination of \vsini{} is not
dominant in the subgiant regime.

The observation that the rapid rotator fraction is smaller for photometric
rotation periods than \vsini{} for the subgiants is expected near the location
of the Kraft break. Hotter stars transition to having radiative envelopes and 
reduced surface magnetic fields, which substantially decreases the starspot
amplitudes. As a result, while most of the hot sample are likely genuine rapid
rotators, only a fraction of them can be detected from starspot modulation. 

One astrophysical background of rapid rotators we expect to persist on the
subgiant branch is a tidally-synchronized binary population on the level of 2\%
with orbital periods at least to 7 days \citep{,Simonian19}. As seen from the merger limits in 
\cref{fig:vsini_bins}, most of this population is expected to persist up 
to the giant branch. Because the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction across
the subgiants is substantially larger than 2\%, we claim that these
rapidly-rotating population are genuine single-star rotators. 


Confirming that the APOGEE \vsini{} yield appropriate results in the subgiant
regime, we can use the \vsini{} to validate broad trends in rotation predicted
by stellar angular momentum models \citep{vanSaders13}. It's observed that
massive stars above the Kraft break rotate extremely quickly on the main
sequence \citep{Kraft67,Abt95}. As these stars evolve onto the subgiant branch, 
one of the general predictions of angular momentum evolution models are 
that rapid rotation can actually persist quite far along the subgiant branch.
In fact, at fixed \Teff{}, stars of higher mass are expected to have larger
rotation rates (see Fig. 2 in \citet{vanSaders13}). 

We compare the rapid rotator fraction to predictions of stellar spindown on the
subgiant branch from \citet{vanSaders13} in \cref{fig:rapidfracs}. 
\citet{vanSaders13} calculated solar-metallicity evolutionary tracks using two 
bounding sets of initial conditions: the slow and rapid envelopes on cluster rotational 
sequences (see \citet{vanSaders13} Section 2.2 for more details). For each 
launch condition, we plot an ``iso-velocity'' boundary: the location on each 
track where the evolved star reaches a rotational velocity of 10 \kms. Regions 
to the left of the slow launch model should have high spectroscopic rapid
rotator fractions, and transition to zero at the fast launch edge. 

\begin{figure*}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{binned_rapid_rotators}
    \caption{Rapid rotator fraction as a function of \Teff{} for four different
    bins in luminosity. The value of the slow and fast-launch model in the
center of each bin is shown as pink vertical lines (dashed and solid). The
range of model values within a bin is shown as the shaded region. The maximum
and minimum rapid rotator fractions are shown as a horizontal dotted lines.}
    \label{fig:rrbins}
\end{figure*}

We quantify the agreement between the models and the data by comparing the
temperature where the rapid rotator fraction drops at fixed luminosity. The
rapid rotator fraction is shown in \cref{fig:rrbins} in four bins of absolute
magnitude and equally-populated bins of temperature which span the observed 
subgiant domain. The predicted range of the slow- and fast-launch boundaries 
of the \citet{vanSaders13} models in each bin are shown as pink shaded regions. 

We find that the models are consistent with the data in the most luminous bins. 
The rapid rotator fractions reach zero before the cool edge of the fast launch
condition. In the least luminous bin, the rapid rotator fraction does not
monotonically decrease, but rises and falls erratically. This behavior is
likely not related to the models, but likely due to the photometric binary
sequence extending observed in \cref{fig:rapidfracs} into the hot dwarf regime.

On the lower main sequence, we also see a discrepancy where the spectroscopic 
rapid rotator fraction is substantially larger than the photometric rapid 
rotator fractions. Unlike with the subgiants, where we argued that the
photometric rapid rotator rate is suppressed by decreased spot amplitudes, we 
argue here that photometric 
rapid rotator fraction is more representative of the true rapid rotator 
fraction because of the blending due to spectral lines noted in 
Section~\ref{sec:binaries}. The detection fraction for starspots is not expected
to drop dramatically in this regime like it did with the subgiants.
Additionally, all of the spectroscopic rapid rotators are location on the
photometric binary sequence, where spectral blending should have the largest
impact.

\subsubsection{\(\alpha\)-rich Blue Stragglers}

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{High_Alpha_Turnoff}
    \caption{The \(\alpha\)-rich APOGEE sample with \(\mgfe > 0.2\). A
        representative error bar is shown in the upper right corner. The halo
    turnoff can be seen in this sample, next to a 10 Gyr \(\feh = -0.25\) MIST
isochrone for illustration. Stars with \(\vsini > 10 \kms\) are shown as red 
circles.} 
    \label{fig:highalpha}
\end{figure}

The \Kepler{} field also contains a small population of halo stars, which can
be distinguished by their high \(\alpha\) abundance, with magnesium providing
the best discrimination \citep{Hayes18}. This population is thought to be old 
with a kinematic age of order 10 Gyr \citep{Schuster12}. We isolate these 
stars by imposing a cut of \(\mgfe > 0.2\). The \(\alpha\)-rich stars are shown
in \cref{fig:highalpha}.

In the high \(\alpha\) sequence, we find clear evidence of a halo turnoff sequence,
which is approximately traced by a 10 Gyr \(\feh = -0.25\) MIST isochrone. We
contend that the stars hotter than the field turnoff are halo blue stragglers,
which have a high fraction of spectroscopic rapid rotators. Just like with the
low-\(\alpha\) population, these hot, luminous stars are rotating rapidly.
However, due to their great age, these stars are not rotating rapidly due to 
youth, but rather because of their merger history.

We also note the possible detection of a Kraft Break for the halo population.
The Kraft Break manifests as an abrupt transition between rapid and slow 
rotators caused by the development of surface convection driving magnetized 
winds. We observe an abrupt change in the rotation properties of blue
stragglers based on initial mass. We illustrate this dichotomy by plotting a
1.18 M\(_\sun\) \(\feh = -0.25\) MIST evolutionary track and noting that blue
stragglers more massive than this track are nearly all rapid rotators, and
those less massive are slow rotators.

The spectroscopic \vsini{} provided by APOGEE is a very powerful tool for
probing rotation at non-solar compositions. While this study was restricted to
the \Kepler{} field due to the availability of both photometric and
spectroscopic rotation rates, taking advantage of \vsini{} over the full sky
will yield larger samples across a wide range of metallicities which can probe
the behavior of rotation beyond what is accessible with young open clusters.


\subsection{Sub-subgiants}

Finally, we note the presence of a nodule of rapid rotators extending down from the
giant branch at \(\Teff \sim 4400\) K and \(2 > \MK > 0\). We posit that these
are the field equivalent of sub-subgiants---tidally-synchronized evolved
subgiants which are anomalously active due to their high spin rate
\citep{Geller17,Leiner17}. 

\subsection{Photometric Binaries and \vsini{}}
\label{sec:binaries}

As noted in the Pleiades and \Kepler{} field, APOGEE \vsini{} values are
discordant for a fraction of photometric binaries. In the Pleiades, we
calculated this fraction to be \(15^{+12}_{-8.1}\%\). In the \Kepler{} field,
we found that the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction is \(30^{+6.0}_{-5.7}\)
(see \cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf}),
over the predicted fraction of \(8.8^{+3.8}_{-2.8}\), for an excess of around
21\%, which is consistent with that seen in the Pleiades.

We interpret the anomalously high APOGEE \vsini{} as the APOGEE spectrograph
misclassifying unresolved binaries as rapid rotators. This would only occur for
binary systems for which the RV offset between the components \(\Delta v \sin 
    i = \textrm{c}/\mathcal{R} \sim 14 \kms\) is comparable to the velocity resolution
of the spectrograph. This mechanism would predict that there would be a
sharp transition in \vsini{} between this contamination of rapid rotators and
stars labeled as resolved spectroscopic binaries. As is evident from
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, there is a transition \vsini{} between
these inconsistent rapid rotators and the double-lined spectroscopic binaries.
This type of contamination is most prevalent in the cool \Kepler{} dwarfs 
because true rapid rotators are intrinsically rare. In rapidly-rotating 
populations, such as the Pleiades, the rotational broadening of most stars is
already on the order of the instrumental resolution, hence the discrepancy
between the photometric and spectroscopic rotation measures would be much
smaller in these cases.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \plotone{phot_cont}
    \caption{Distribution of RV offsets for a population of \(0.5 M_\sun\)
    equal-mass binaries in circular orbits following a \citet{Raghavan10}
    period distribution. A Monte-carlo realization is shown as the light gray
    histogram while an analytic expression is shown as the thick black line.
    The red region denotes the range between \(7 \kms < \Delta v < 14 \kms\),
    which is expected to correspond to the case of unresolved spectral blends.
    Around 10\% of the sample falls in between this range.
\label{fig:phot_cont}}
\end{figure}

We make an order-of-magnitude estimate of the rate of this phenomenon for a 
sample of photometric binaries. We build a toy model of a population of
equal-mass \(0.5 M_\sun\) binaries in circular orbits with a period 
distribution given by the lognormal distribution in \citet{Raghavan10}. We 
model the radial velocity difference of this population by drawing 1,000,000 
randomly distributed periods, inclinations, and orbital phase using Monte 
Carlo techniques. The histogram along with the analytic estimate is shown in
\cref{fig:phot_cont}. To estimate the rate of spectral blending, we assume that
all systems where the RV offset is between 0.5--1 times the resolution of the
APOGEE spectrograph would correspond to spectral blends, which yields a rate of
10\% in our model photometric binary population. This value is consistent with
the 15\% measured in the Pleiades, and close to the 21\% observed in the
\Kepler{} field.

We emphasize that this calculation is a very rough estimate, and did not take
into account important additional effects. First, the asserted boundaries
causing spurious \vsini{} between 0.5--1 times the resolution of the 
spectrograph are more complex in reality, and will depend sensitively on the 
signal-to-noise of the
spectrum as well as the cadence of observations in the case of multiple visits.
Additionally, eccentricity will adjust the distribution in a nontrivial way, on
one hand increasing the range of RV offsets per system, but also skewing the 
distribution of RV offsets toward smaller values. Additional modeling with the
ASPCAP pipeline is required to truly estimate the rate of spectral blends,
which is beyond the scope of this work.
This hypothesis can be tested with high-resolution spectroscopic follow-up of
these broad-lined targets to check if they decompose into two narrow components.

\subsection{Regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}


A complicating factor is the possible presence of spurious \vsini{}
measurements photometric binaries as highlighted in the Pleiades. Such binaries 
will be relatively common for an unevolved primary, but less common for an 
evolved one. To simplify the interpretation of the rotation distribution
for each of these populations, we partition our sample into regimes where
these populations can be isolated.

The cool dwarf regime is defined as the region on the main sequence where
stars have not evolved substantially compared to the age of the galaxy. As a
result, the single stars can be easily separated from the photometric binaries. 
We base the temperature threshold (\(\Teff < 5250\) K) for the 
cool dwarf regime both on empirical measurements of the main sequence width, 
as well as predictions from stellar isochrones as discussed in 
\citet{Simonian19}. We draw a luminosity threshold of 1.3 mag above a 1 Gyr 
solar metallicity isochrone from the MESA Isochrones and Stellar Tracks 
\citep[MIST]{Dotter16,Choi16} model version 1.2, to separate the 
main-sequence from the photometric binaries. As noted in
\citet{Simonian19}, the cool dwarfs are largely devoid of rapid rotators,
as would be expected from an old stellar population. 

More luminous than the cool dwarfs, spanning 0.3 and 1.3 magnitudes above the
isochrone are the photometric binaries. This regime is expected to contain
genuine rotators, but also may contain targets with spuriously high \vsini{} as
noted in the Pleiades.

The hot dwarf regime is defined where age and binarity effects are degenerate, 
at \(\Teff > 5250\) K and located less than 1.3 mag above the MIST isochrone. 
In this regime it is ambiguous whether a
star more luminous than the main sequence is an evolved single star with a true
radius larger than on the main sequence, or a photometric binary that should 
be deprojected. 

At higher luminosities, binarity becomes less prominent. As a binary system
evolves, the primary increases in luminosity more rapidly than the secondary,
reducing the relative contribution of the companion's light. 
We define a subgiant regime ranging from 1.3 -- 4.5 mag above the main 
sequence on the hot end of the regime. The maximum luminosity of the sample 
separates it from the red clump. The high luminosities of 
these targets imply that they must be descended from high-mass stars more 
massive than the Kraft break, and they should yield useful insights into the
evolution of angular momentum across the subgiant branch.

All other stars are considered to be giants. We don't examine them as part of
this work. Previous work have focused on rapid rotation in the APOKASC giant
sample \citep{Tayar15}.

  \centering
  \plotone{regimes}
  \caption{The APOGEE targets subdivided into four regimes: cool 
      dwarfs (purple), hot dwarfs (mustard), subgiants (teal), and giants 
      (red). The separation between hot and cool samples is at

      \(\Delta \MK = -1.2 \textrm{ and } -4.75\). Also shown as pink lines are

          0.0 \textrm{ (solid), and } 0.5 
  \textrm{ (dotted).}\)\label{fig:regimes}}

The raw distribution of spectroscopic rapid rotators on the HR diagram shown in
\cref{fig:vsini_bins} already demonstrates important structure. As noted 
in \citet{Simonian19}, there are many rapid rotators in the photometric binary 
regime, consistent with a synchronized binary population with almost none in 
the single stars. We also note the presence of a substantial number of 
rapidly rotating subgiants.

Along with the regimes on the HR diagram, we use the asteroseismic dwarfs as an
additional test sample. The radii of these stars are measured extremely
precisely, and the periods are measured by \citet{Garcia14} using an
independent method from \citet{McQuillan14}. Any intrinsic problems with the
spectroscopic \vsini{} would manifest themselves in this sample.

Use of the APOGEE \vsini{} in the dwarf and subgiant regime is relatively 
novel, and so we dedicate a more
detailed analysis to characterize those measurements, along with potential
impacts of binarity on these observations. We compare APOGEE
\vsini{} to literature values from high-resolution spectra in order to
determine the detection threshold and uncertainty of the \vsini{}. 

\subsubsection{Cool Dwarfs and Photometric Binaries}
\label{sec:cooldwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \plotone{Cool_Dwarf_vsini_veq_comparison}
  \caption{Same as \cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}, except photometric binaries are
      marked in green. The radius is determined by deprojecting targets onto a 
      MIST isochrone.\label{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}}
\end{figure}

For the cool dwarfs and photometric binaries, we compare the two rotational 
measures using the \citet{McQuillan14} periods, the MIST-derived radii, and 
the APOGEE \vsini{}. The comparison between the \vsini{} and equatorial velocity is shown
in \cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}. Visual SB2s behave 
as expected; the ASPCAP pipeline attempts to fit the two lines as a single 
peak and incidentally measures a high \vsini{}. The photometric binaries 
25/27 of the rapid rotators, which is consistent with rapid rotation being
associated with binaries \citep{Simonian19}. However, there is a large 
disagreement in both the rapid rotator rates and in the values themselves. 

For nearly all of the spectroscopic rapid rotators and the photometric 
binaries, the \vsini{} is too large given the observed period and radius. 
Additionally, as shown in 
\cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf}, the rapid rotator fraction as observed with \vsini{} is 
greater than the raw photometric rapid rotation rate by a factor of 3, a 
nearly \(4\sigma\) discrepancy for the corrected rotation rate. The fact
that the discrepancy is in the complete opposite direction as the \(\sin i\) 
projection indicates a very severe systematic present in the photometric
binaries, but not seen in the asteroseismic sample.

We also note the presence of high \vsini{} among the population for which
\citet{McQuillan14} did not see a rotation signal. As seen in
\cref{tab:rapidfracdwarf}, 15\% of the sample without significant period
detections appear to have high \vsini{}, which is not compatible with our 
current understanding of rotation-activity relations. Physical reasons for 
stars not having detected photometric modulation include either that the activity is low
enough that the photometric variation due to spots are below the detection
threshold of \Kepler, or that the star is aligned nearly pole-on to us. In
neither case would the \vsini{} be expected to be large.

Because most of the rapid rotators in this regime are photometric binaries,
it's difficult to determine whether this systematic exists in the single cool
dwarfs. There are only two spectroscopic rapid
rotators that are photometric singles, and one lies marginally over the 
\(\sin i = 1\) line and the other over the detection threshold.  As a result, 
there must be some systematic in either the \vsini{}, radius, or
rotation period to yield such a tension between the \vsini{} and rotation
period.

\subsubsection{Hot Dwarfs}
\label{sec:hotdwarfs}

  \centering
  \plotone{Hot_Dwarf_vsini_veq_comparison}
  \caption{Similar to \cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, except for the 
      hot dwarf sample. Stars which are not photometric singles are
      classified by \citet{ElBadry18b} and labeled as squares.
      \citet{ElBadry18b} singles are shown in black. \citet{ElBadry18b} SB2s

In the hot star domain we can also separate photometric singles from stars
consistent with being photometric binaries, keeping in mind that the 
photometric binary category will contain a substantial sample of genuine 
evolved single stars. Because displacement above the main sequence is
ambiguous, We include the classifications of \citet{ElBadry18b}
for stars which are more luminous than the photometric binary limit.
Stars which were not analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b} are
labeled separately. 

We plot the equatorial velocity against the measured \vsini{} for the hot 
dwarf sample in the left panel of \cref{fig:hot_dwarf_velocity_comparison}.
Unlike the cool dwarf sample, the hot dwarfs contain many more single rapid
rotators, which is to be expected because they have many stars from above the
Kraft break. We can see that both the photometric singles, and 
\citet{ElBadry18b} singles, lie in the physically reasonable regime of this 
diagram, where \(\sin i \le 1\). Stars which are more luminous than the
photometric binary boundary, on the other hand, are much 
more likely to appear in the forbidden region of this diagram, which
indicates that photometric binarity is correlated with disagreements between 
spectroscopic and photometric rapid rotation. 

When we compare the spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction
(\(15.8^{+2.0}_{-1.8}\)) to the photometric rapid rotator fraction
(\(15.9^{+1.9}_{-1.7}\)), we find that they agree well within \(1\sigma\). We
conclude that APOGEE \vsini{} is generally reliable in this regime.

\subsubsection{Subgiants and RGB Base}
\label{sec:evolvedstars}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \plotone{Subgiant_vsini_veq_comparison}
  \caption{Same as \cref{fig:hot_dwarf_velocity_comparison}, except for the
      subgiant sample.\label{fig:subgiant_velocity_comparison}}
\end{figure}

We lastly look at the confluence between the two rotation measures in the
subgiants. Unlike in the area near the main sequence, \citet{McQuillan14} 
actively attempted to eliminate subgiants in their sample.  As a result, of 
the 979 stars in the subgiants, only 583 were analyzed by 
\citet{McQuillan14}. While the strong selection effects 
imply that the \citet{McQuillan14} periods will not be representative of the 
rotation distribution of the subgiants as a whole, the small overlap sample 
is still useful for checking that spectroscopic and photometric rotation 
measures yield the same answer. 

We show the comparison between the equatorial velocity and \vsini{} in
\cref{fig:subgiant_velocity_comparison}. In this regime, the only binarity
information available is from the \citet{ElBadry18b} criteria
and the presence of visual SB2s. The subgiants have several objects with 
\(\sin i > 1\), all of which haven't been analyzed by \citet{ElBadry18b} for 
the presence of a companion. It would therefore be helpful to independently verify
whether these systems show signs of a companion or not. Nonetheless, the 
concordance in the rapid rotator fractions indicates that this is not a 
dominant background. This result is also consistent with the good
agreement for the asteroseismic sample, which overwhelmingly consists of 
subgiants.

There is no overlap between \vsini{} detections and \citet{McQuillan14} periods
in the RGB base regime. The spectroscopic rapid rotator fraction is nearly
zero, in stark contrast to the photometric rapid rotator fraction, which is
only based on a small fraction of the sample. Interpreting the rotation
distribution in this regime is highly complicated by the fact that
\citet{McQuillan14} actively attempted to remove these stars from their 
analysis, and that this sample is in the region where the ASPCAP pipeline
classifies stars as giants rather than dwarfs, so the spectroscopic rapid
rotator rate is underestimated. To better probe the
\vsini{} distribution in this regime, a more specialized approach, such as
that taken in \citet{Tayar15} will be necessary.



\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The spectroscopic rotation data featured a luminosity and temperature-dependent
boundary between hot, rapid rotators and cool, slow rotators which was also
seen in the photometric rotation period. This trend is likely reflective of
stellar spindown along the subgiant branch, and can be compared to models. We
also discuss studying trends in stellar rotation with abundance using APOGEE
abundances.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared the rotation periods and spectroscopic \vsini{} for a sample of
5466 dwarfs and subgiants observed by APOGEE.\@ By comparing
the \vsini{} to previously observed dwarfs in the Pleiades with high-resolution
spectroscopy, we calibrate the APOGEE \vsini{} and determine a detection 
threshold of 10 \kms{} and fractional uncertainty of around 12\%. We also find 
discrepancies between the APOGEE \vsini{} and rotation periods between 15-21\%
of known photometric binaries.  We propose that the discrepancy among
photometric binaries is likely
due to complex variation from two superimposed signals causing rotational 
periods to be overestimated, or it can be due to intermediate-period 
photometric binaries in an orbital configuration where their RV difference is 
on the order of the APOGEE velocity resolution. This mechanism explains the
similar rate of inconsistencies between literature and APOGEE \vsini{} values 
in the Pleiades and in the \Kepler{} field.

For populations containing evolved stars, the impact of binarity is reduced, 
but not eliminated. Investigations of rotation in heterogeneous stellar populations 
should consider that there will be a smoothly-varying background population of 
spurious rapid rotators for targets more luminous than the photometric binary 
sequence. We advise against making detailed comparisons between models and 
data in the subgiant regime without modeling the effect of binarity, or 
without an efficient way of flagging potential near-equal luminosity binaries. 
The methodology in \citet{ElBadry18b} is promising for filtering binaries with
luminous companions, but needs to be run for the much larger DR14 sample in
order to be useful for the subgiant domain.

After validating that spurious \vsini{} values are not a dominant component in
the subgiants, we compare the distribution of spectroscopic rapid rotators to
those predicted by stellar angular momentum models.  We find that the trends 
of the model boundary match well with the empirical boundary seen in the data. 
We also find strong evidence of a spectroscopic rapid rotator excess near 
the photometric binary sequence in our model comparisons, which is likely an 
artifact of spectral blending.

We also use the abundance measurements in APOGEE to isolate high \afe{} halo
stars. We are able to trace the halo turnoff in the \(\alpha\) rich population
and discern a blue straggler population. Despite the vastly different
rotational histories of the blue stragglers, we find a familiar pattern of the
Kraft break, with stars above a certain mass maintaining rapid rotation, and
stars below quickly losing it. With the validation of APOGEE \vsini{} in the
dwarf and subgiant regime, APOGEE can be an valuable tool for studying rotation
beyond solar abundance.
