\section{Introduction}

One of the significant results from the \Kepler{} satellite has been the
determination of rotational periods for a large sample of old, field stars
\citep{Basri11,Affer12,Nielsen13,Reinhold13,McQuillan14,Garcia14}.
These rotation rates are expected to be extremely useful to deriving a field
gyrochronology, which can lead to a better characterization of the ages of
\Kepler{} stars, and hence a better understanding of the discovered planets.


The \Kepler{} field was designed to point outside of the plane of the galactic
disk, making the field biased toward old stars. Despite the old age of the
field, there is still a population of rapid rotators at the level of 1\% of the
population which have rotation rates significantly higher than expected from
existing gyrochronological relations. One possible explanation for these rapid
rotators are that they are tidally-synchronized binaries. The synchronized
binaries are useful because they provide insight into the formation of binaries
at scales too small for disk fragmentation to occur. 

While there have been many attempts to evaluate the determination of rotation
periods in stars throughout the \Kepler{} field, they have all been through
comparisons between different periodogram analysis, but not with independent 
measures of rotation, such as \vsini.

In this paper, we look at the rotation distribution of \Kepler{} targets as
seen from APOGEE \vsini{}s. APOGEE's limited resolution will only make it
sensitive to the most rapid rotators, particularly on the cool end. However,
this is exactly the population we're interested in. 

\section{Sample Selection}
\label{sec:sample}

We select our sample to minimize ambiguity in the stellar radius when
comparing rotation periods to \vsini. In general, large uncertainties in
determining spectral types propogate to the radius estimates, especially in 
the hot star regime, where a star of a given \Teff{} and \logg{} can have 
multiple different radii due to overlap with higher-mass subgiants.

In order to sidestep detailed modeling of the complex radius uncertainties 
for all \Kepler{} targets, we choose instead to study measures of
stellar rotation
in two regimes where radius uncertainties are greatly reduced: the
APOKASC asteroseismic dwarf/subgiant sample, and the cool \(\Teff < 5500\) K 
dwarf regime. 

The APOKASC asteroseismic dwarf and subgiant sample
consists of 415 targets \gvs{Check this number} which presented
solar-type oscillations in \Kepler{} short-cadence data
\citep{Chaplin11}, and were observed in APOGEE DR13
\citep{Majewski17}. Using asteroseismic scaling relations, the radii
for these objects can be determined to an accuracy of around 3\%
\citep{Serenelli17}. It should be noted that the asteroseismic sample
is highly biased compared to the field population. Subgiants are
overrepresented because the
amplitude of solar-type oscillations increases as stars evolve. 
Additionally, the nondetection
of oscillations in rapidly-rotating giants that ought to be detectable
indicates that the asteroseismic sample may be biased against rapid
rotators \citep{Gaulme14,Tayar15}.

We selected 426 asteroseismic dwarfs from the APOKASC catalog v4.2.4. To 
obtain a high-quality sample for further study, we removed two objects with 
the STAR\_BAD flag because they tended to be cases with bad subtraction or
normalization in the ASPCAP pipeline. We also set aside objects with either
of the STAR\_WARN or VSINI\_WARN flags for further inspection to ensure that 
their ASPCAP
stellar parameters are reliable. 93 of
these stars have periods in \citet{McQuillan14}. The combined 77 APOGEE spectra without
quality flags were
also inspected by eye for double-lined spectra, which excluded two
(2M19252467+4207248/KIC6689943 \& 2M19034695+4055143/KIC5689219) objects. 
None of the final sample of 75 objects exhibit stable variability in
their light curves indicative of pulsations. The remaining asteroseismic 
dwarfs are shown in blue in \cref{fig:targeting}

We define the cool star regime as the region in
the HR-diagram of \citet{Huber14} parameters, where the subgiant branch for
stars older than the field turnoff are well-separated from the
main-sequence. The separation is prominent for \(T < 5450\) K with a
dividing line at \(\log g = 4.3\), as illustrated in 
\cref{fig:targeting}. In this regime, uncertainties
in the radius must be due to uncertainties in the \logg{}, not
ambiguities in the mass. Additionally, stellar evolution occurs slowly enough
in this mass range that the change in radius over typical ages of these stars 
is expected to be small.

We restrict ourselves to a known, well-defined sample, by selecting
objects with the \texttt{APOGEE\_KEPLER\_COOLDWARF} flag. They were
originally targeted as a magnitude-limited same with having original KIC 
\citep{Brown11} \(\Teff < 5500\) K and 
\(\logg > 4.0\)\footnote{\url{http://www.sdss.org/dr12/algorithms/ancillary/apogee/kepler_dwarfs/}}. 
We include a few targets on the original list which were observed later
in APOGEE2 under the targeting flag \texttt{APOGEE2\_APOKASC\_DWARF}. We also 
perform the same quality checks as with the
asteroseismic sample: remove STAR\_BAD targets and check for
double-lined spectra. The cool dwarfs are shown in red in
\cref{fig:targeting}.

\section{\vsini{} Comparison}

The analysis performed in this manuscript relies heavily on
measurements of rotation period, stellar radius, and \vsini. Because we
adopt rotation periods from \citet{McQuillan14}, we refer the
reader to that work, as well as to \citet{Aigrain15} for a detailed 
discussion of the quality of their and other period-finding algorithms.

As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:sample}, the asteroseismic stars are a
benchmark on how precisely stellar radii can be measured. For a detailed
discussion of the uncertainties of using grid-based asteroseismic
scaling relations for the APOKASC dwarfs, we refer the reader to
\citet{Serenelli17}. 

For the cool dwarf sample, we use radii reported in the Kepler Stellar 
Properties
Catalog\footnote{\url{https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/docs/Kepler_stellar_docs.html}}
\citep[a.k.a.\ revised KIC]{Mathur17}, derived from DSEP isochrones 
\citep{Dotter08} based on the methodology of \citet{Huber14}. 
However, objects with \(5500 \textrm{ K } > \Teff > 5000 \textrm{ K }\) are 
specially denoted as having uncertain radii due to confusion with subgiants.

To measure the accuracy of \vsini{} for asteroseismic targets, we
compare APOGEE \vsini{}s to those measured for a sample of asteroseismic
dwarfs in \citet{Bruntt12} \gvs{See if there's overlap with
  \citet{Bruntt10a}}. \citet{Bruntt12} analyzed spectra of 93
solar-type asteroseismic targets using two R=80,000 spectrographs on the
3.6-meter Canada--France--Hawaii Telescope and 2-m Bernard Lyot
Telescope. While uncertainties are not quoted in \citet{Bruntt12},
previous analyses have determined \vsini{} uncertainties of about 0.6
\kms \citep{Bruntt10a,Bruntt10b}.

A comparison between the APOGEE and \citet{Bruntt12} targets is shown in
\cref{fig:bruntt_vsini}. This figure indicates that the scatter is
discontinuous at \citep{Bruntt12} \(\vsini=7\kms\). We interpret this
discontinuity as setting the detection limit of APOGEE, 
where stellar rotation is actually detected in the high-\vsini{} regime.
The scatter in the fractional error is consistent with 10\% for the
detections, so we adopt that as the uncertainty in the APOGEE \vsini{} values.

Of these five objects, three (KIC 8677016, 8952800, 10518551) have
\vsini{} values close to the detection threshold. Because the errors of 
marginal \vsini{} detections are complex, 
it may be possible that these are outliers. KIC 3223000 has a
\vsini{} which is slightly above the predicted equatorial velocity. It
is a 1.7-\(\sigma\) difference based on the \vsini{} uncertainty, which
is consistent with being a statistical fluctuation. Additionally, it can
be explained by 4.9 day measured rotation period actually being a 
half-period detection alias, implying that the real equatorial velocity to be twice 
as large. 

The final outlier, KIC 9908400, is so significantly off of the relation
that it can not be explained by either uncertainties or aliasing. However,
it may be adequately explained by contamination of the \Kepler{} PSF\@.
KIC 9908400 has a \Teff{} which places it above the Kraft break. If the 
photometric variation originated in a cooler star within 
the Kepler PSF, the mismatch between the period and \vsini{} would
be explained.

To see if this mismatch is prevalent among the hot stars because they
have much weaker magnetic fields (van Saders, in prep), we checked 
if the velocity mismatch trends with effective temperature.
\Cref{fig:velmatch_teff} compares the predicted equatorial velocities to
\vsini{} for objects with varying effective temperatures. There's no
apparent trend where hotter stars tend to be more mismatched. However,
this does not preclude KIC 9908400 from being a contaminant if this sort
of contamination is rare.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{apokasc_vsini_comparison}
  \caption{\emph{Top:} Observed distribution of \vsini{} (red) compared
  to the theoretical distribution of \vsini{} (black). The theoretical
distribution was obtained by taking the inferred equatorial velocity
distribution (blue) and convolving it with \(\sin i\) and a 10\%
uncertainty. \vsini{} non-detections are treated separately; out of 75 
objects, 43 non-detections were predicted and 42 were observed.
\emph{Bottom:} Cumulative distribution of \vsini{} including the impact
of non-detections. Small statistical blips are seen around 15 and 20
km/s, but according to a KS test, these two distributions are consistent

The overall distribution of \vsini{} is consistent with the inferred
velocity distribution. We tested this by taking the velocity
distribution inferred from the periods, convolving it with a Gaussian of 
width 10\%, and then convolving that distribution with a distribution of
randomly-oriented \(\sin i\) \gvs{I should cutoff the \(\sin i\)
distribution at \(1/2\) instead of 0, since \(\sin i < 1/2\) won't be
seen if there are spots.} The results are shown in
\cref{fig:apokasc_vdists}. As expected, the number of targets in each
\vsini{} bin tracks the theoretical prediction, including non-detections. To 
quantify this agreement, a KS-test indicated that the \vsini{}s were 
consistent with being drawn from the theoretical distribution at the
\(p=0.5\) confidence level. We note that the  convolutions were performed 
on the binned dataset as opposed to the full distribution function, which 
introduced 
integration errors on the order of 2\%. However, decreasing the bin sizes 
didn't affect the conclusion.

\subsection{Cool Dwarfs}

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{cool_stars_velocity_radius}
  \caption{\emph{Left:} ASPCAP \vsini{} compared to the inferred
equatorial velocity for cool APOKASC dwarfs with vsini detections. 5/27
objects have \vsini{} values larger than the inferred equatorial
velocities. \emph{Right:} Asteroseismic radii compared to radii inferred from
\vsini{} and the rotation period. The points above the line are the same
as the points above the line on the left.\label{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}}
\end{figure}

In this sample, the \vsini{}, period, and radius indicates heavy 
disagreement, as seen in
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}. For a large number of objects
the \vsini{} is too large given the observed period and radius. The
disagreement is significantly more severe for the dwarfs than the
subgiants. Therefore, we explore what could go wrong in radius, \vsini{}, or
rotation period.

One of the obvious places where things can go wrong is with the stellar
radii. Subgiants misclassified as dwarfs would have equatorial
velocities that are severely underestimated by the \citet{Huber14}
pipeline. While misclassified subgiants wouldn't be able to explain the
objects with very large discrepancies in
\cref{fig:cool_stars_velocity_radius}, it could plausibly be behind the 
objects with \(R\sin i \sim 2 R_\sun\).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{cool_apogee_logg_comparison}
  \caption{Dwarfs and subgiants classified by \citet{Huber14} placed in
  an HR diagram with APOGEE spectroscopic parameters. Most of the 
  \citet{Huber14} dwarfs (blue crosses) are on the APOGEE dwarf locus.
  The \citet{Huber14} subgiants (red diamonds) are found both in the
  subgiant branch as well as the dwarf locus. \vsini{} detections are
  denoted as squares, with a magenta fill indicating a \citet{Huber14}
  subgiant and a white fill indicating a \citet{Huber14} 
  dwarf.\label{fig:cool_apogee_logg_comparison}}
\end{figure}

In order to determine whether this misclassification could be an issue,
we compare the dwarf/subgiant classification from \citet{Huber14} to
that which would be obtained from APOGEE spectroscopic \logg{}
measurements. While the spectroscopic \logg{} measurements for dwarfs
are uncalibrated, and should not be taken at face value, we assume that
they should be able to distinguish between subgiants and dwarfs, at the
very least. The APOGEE spectroscopic measurements are shown in
\cref{fig:cool_apogee_logg_comparison}. Due to the uncalibrated nature
of the APOGEE \logg{}s, the main sequence does not behave as expected,
particularly at the cool end. However, a clear main-sequence and
subgiant branch can be distinguished. The APOGEE measurements indicate
the surprising result that it is not the subgiants that are
misclassified as dwarfs by \citet{Huber14}; rather APOGEE indicates that
dwarfs are being misclassified as subgiants. This exacerbates the
problem of the radii being underestimated.
